Introduction to Whole Numbers | A digit's {place value} depends on its position. In 352, the 5 represents five tens, or 50. | The {whole numbers} are 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on. They do not include fractions or negative numbers. | To {round} a number, inspect the digit to the right of the chosen place. For example, 352 rounds to 350 to the nearest ten.
Add and Subtract Fractions | Before adding fractions with different denominators, rewrite them using a {common denominator}. For 1/2 and 1/3, use 6. | An {equivalent fraction} names the same amount. Multiplying both parts of 1/2 by 3 gives 3/6. | After finding a common denominator, add the {numerators} and keep the denominator. Thus 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.
Systems of Measurement | A {unit} tells what a measurement counts. A length of 3 meters is not the same measurement as 3 centimeters. | A {conversion factor} is a ratio of equal measurements, such as 100 centimeters divided by 1 meter. Its value is one. | In {dimensional analysis}, arrange conversion factors so unwanted units cancel. Multiplying 2 meters by 100 centimeters per meter gives 200 centimeters.
Solve Equations Using the Subtraction and Addition Properties of Equality | An equation stays balanced when you perform the {same operation} on both sides. Adding 3 to only one side changes the equation. | To solve x + 7 = 12, {subtract} 7 from each side. The result is x = 5. | Use {substitution} to check a solution. Replacing x with 5 in x + 7 = 12 gives 12 = 12.
Use a General Strategy to Solve Linear Equations | A {solution} is a value that makes an equation true. For 2x + 3 = 11, that value is 4. | First {simplify} each side by distributing and combining like terms. Then collect variable terms on one side and constants on the other. | To {isolate} x in 2x + 3 = 11, subtract 3 from both sides, then divide both sides by 2.
Solve Linear Inequalities | An {inequality} compares values rather than stating they are equal. For example, x < 5 includes every number below 5. | When multiplying or dividing both sides by a {negative} number, reverse the inequality sign. From -2x < 6, obtain x > -3. | An {open circle} on a number line excludes an endpoint. A filled circle includes it, as in x ≤ 5.
Use a Problem-Solving Strategy | Identify the {unknown} and assign it a variable before writing an equation. Include the units that your answer will need. | Translate the relationship into an {equation}. If three equal tickets cost $24, write 3t = 24, so each ticket costs $8. | Always {check} the result against the original situation. A mathematically valid answer may still be impossible, such as a negative number of tickets.
Solve Geometry Applications: Triangles, Rectangles, and the Pythagorean Theorem | A rectangle's {area} is length times width. A 4-meter by 3-meter rectangle covers 12 square meters. | A polygon's {perimeter} is the total distance around its boundary. That same rectangle has perimeter 4 + 3 + 4 + 3 = 14 meters. | The {Pythagorean theorem} applies to right triangles: a² + b² = c². The side c is opposite the right angle.
Solve Applications with Linear Inequalities | Translate a limit into an {inequality}. A budget of at most $30 can be written as cost ≤ 30. | A {constraint} is a condition that an answer must satisfy. Buying whole tickets also means that the ticket count must be a nonnegative integer. | Test the {boundary} to decide whether it belongs in the solution. At most includes equality; strictly less than does not.
Use the Rectangular Coordinate System | An {ordered pair} (x, y) gives a point's horizontal coordinate first and vertical coordinate second. The order matters. | The {origin} is where the axes meet, at (0, 0). Moving right increases x; moving upward increases y. | A point is on a graph if its coordinates {satisfy} the equation. The point (2, 5) lies on y = 2x + 1.
Understand Slope of a Line | A line's {slope} is change in y divided by change in x. Between (1, 3) and (3, 7), it is 4/2 = 2. | A {horizontal} line has slope zero because its y-value does not change as x changes. | A vertical line has {undefined} slope because calculating its slope would require division by zero. It does not have slope zero.
Graphs of Linear Inequalities | A two-variable linear inequality describes a {half-plane}: the points on one side of a boundary line, sometimes including that line. | Draw a {dashed} boundary for < or > because points on the line are excluded. Use a solid boundary for ≤ or ≥. | Use a {test point} not on the boundary to choose which side to shade. Substitute its coordinates into the inequality.
Solve Systems of Equations by Graphing | A {system} contains equations that must hold at the same time. Its solution must satisfy every equation. | Two lines' {intersection} gives the solution shared by both equations. For y = x and y = 2 - x, it is (1, 1). | Distinct {parallel} lines never meet, so their system has no solution. Identical lines instead have infinitely many solutions.
Solve Applications with Systems of Equations | Use two {variables} when a problem has two unknown quantities, such as adult tickets and student tickets sold. | Translate separate facts into separate {equations}. Total tickets gives one equation; total revenue can give another. | After solving, {interpret} both numbers using the original quantities and units. Check both equations, not just one.
Graphing Systems of Linear Inequalities | The solution of a system of inequalities is the {overlap} of all its shaded regions. Every inequality must be satisfied. | A point in the {feasible region} meets all constraints. A point outside any one shaded region is not a solution. | Check whether a {boundary line} is included before accepting a point on it. A dashed line excludes its points.
Solve Mixture and Uniform Motion Applications | For constant speed, {distance} equals rate times time. Traveling at 60 miles per hour for 2 hours covers 120 miles. | In a mixture problem, track the amount of {pure substance}. Two liters of a 30% solution contain 0.6 liters of that substance. | Use {consistent units} before building equations. Convert minutes to hours if the rate is measured in miles per hour.
Solve Absolute Value Inequalities | An {absolute value} measures distance from zero, so it cannot be negative. Both 3 and -3 have absolute value 3. | The inequality absolute value of x < 4 uses {and}: x > -4 and x < 4. Its graph is one interval. | The inequality absolute value of x > 4 uses {or}: x < -4 or x > 4. Its graph has two separate rays.
Graph Linear Equations in Two Variables | A {linear equation} in two variables graphs as a straight line. Every point on that line is a solution. | To find an {x-intercept}, set y equal to zero and solve for x. To find a y-intercept, set x equal to zero. | In {slope-intercept form}, y = mx + b, m is slope and b is the y-intercept. For y = 2x + 3, they are 2 and 3.
Graph Linear Inequalities in Two Variables | A linear inequality's {boundary} is the line formed by replacing its inequality sign with an equal sign. | Use a {solid line} for ≤ or ≥. Those signs allow points on the boundary to be part of the solution. | After drawing the boundary, {shade} the side whose points satisfy the inequality. Check a point away from the line to choose correctly.
Graphs of Functions | A {function} gives exactly one output for each allowed input. Different inputs may still share the same output. | The {vertical line test} checks a graph: if any vertical line crosses it more than once, it does not represent y as a function of x. | The {domain} is the set of allowed inputs. For y = 1/x, zero is excluded because division by zero is undefined.
Add and Subtract Polynomials | Terms are {like terms} when their variable parts, including exponents, match. Thus 3x² and -5x² can be combined. | To add polynomials, combine the {coefficients} of like terms. For example, 3x² + 2x² = 5x², not 5x⁴. | When subtracting a polynomial, {distribute} the minus sign to every term. Subtracting (2x - 3) means adding -2x + 3.
Multiply Polynomials | Use the {distributive property} to multiply each term of one polynomial by each term of the other. Include every pair. | When multiplying powers with the same base, {add} their exponents. For example, x² times x³ equals x⁵. | After multiplying, {combine like terms}. Expanding (x + 2)(x + 3) gives x² + 3x + 2x + 6 = x² + 5x + 6.
Dividing Polynomials | Polynomial {long division} repeatedly divides leading terms, multiplies the divisor, and subtracts. Continue until the remaining degree is smaller than the divisor's. | A {remainder} is what is left after division. The dividend equals divisor times quotient plus remainder. | Use {synthetic division} for a divisor of the form x - c. Missing powers must still be represented by zero coefficients.
Simplify Expressions with Roots | A {square root} of a nonnegative number is a value whose square gives that number. The radical symbol names the nonnegative root. | A {perfect square} can be taken out of a square root. Thus √12 = √(4 × 3) = 2√3. | Over the {real numbers}, an even root cannot have a negative quantity inside it. An odd root can.
Solve Quadratic Equations Using the Square Root Property | If x² = 9, both 3 and -3 are {solutions}. Squaring either value gives 9. | First {isolate} the squared expression. From (x - 2)² = 16, take both square roots to get x - 2 = ±4. | The {plus-or-minus} symbol keeps both branches of the solution. Here x = 6 or x = -2; checking confirms both.
Solve Quadratic Inequalities | Begin by finding the quadratic's {zeros}. They divide the number line into intervals where the expression may have different signs. | Choose a {test value} in each interval to determine where the quadratic is positive or negative. Do not assume the sign alternates at every zero. | Include an {endpoint} only when equality is allowed and the expression is defined there. Strict inequalities exclude the zeros.
Finding Composite and Inverse Functions | A {composition} puts one function's output into another function. In f(g(x)), apply g first, then f. | An {inverse} reverses a function's input-output pairs. A function needs to be one-to-one on its domain to have an inverse function. | Check inverse functions by {composing} them in both orders. On the appropriate domains, f(g(x)) and g(f(x)) should each equal x.
Evaluate and Graph Logarithmic Functions | A {logarithm} answers an exponent question. Since 2³ = 8, log base 2 of 8 equals 3. | The {domain} of log base b of x is x > 0, where b > 0 and b ≠ 1. | Logarithmic and exponential functions are {inverses}. Their graphs reflect across y = x when the same base is used.
Solve Exponential and Logarithmic Equations | Use {equal bases} when possible. If 2 raised to x equals 2 raised to 5, then x = 5. | A {logarithm} can bring an unknown exponent into a form you can solve. Taking logs is useful when the bases cannot be matched easily. | Always check the {domain} in the original equation. A proposed solution that makes a logarithm's argument zero or negative must be rejected.
Functions and Function Notation | A {function} assigns each allowed input exactly one output. If f(x) = 2x + 1, then f(3) = 7. | The {domain} is the set of allowed inputs. For f(x) = 1/x, zero is not an allowed input. | The {range} is the set of outputs a function actually produces. For f(x) = x² with real inputs, outputs are nonnegative.
Composition of Functions | A {composition} applies one function after another. In f(g(x)), calculate g(x) first and then apply f. | The {order} of composition matters. If f(x) = x + 1 and g(x) = 2x, then f(g(x)) = 2x + 1 but g(f(x)) = 2x + 2. | The {domain} must work for both stages. An input must be allowed for the inner function, and its output must be allowed for the outer function.
Inverse Functions | An {inverse} function reverses the original function's input-output pairs. If f(2) = 7, then its inverse sends 7 back to 2. | A {one-to-one} function never sends two different inputs to the same output. This condition allows its inverse to be a function. | To check inverses, use {composition}: each function must undo the other on the relevant domain, returning the original input.
Linear Functions | A {linear function} has a constant rate of change. Its graph is a straight line rather than a curve. | In f(x) = mx + b, m is the {slope}. It tells how much the output changes when the input increases by one. | The {intercept} b is the output when x = 0. For f(x) = 3x + 5, the graph crosses the vertical axis at 5.
Modeling with Linear Functions | A {linear model} describes a relationship with a constant rate of change. A fixed starting fee plus a per-mile charge is one example. | The {slope} carries units. If cost is measured in dollars and distance in miles, slope is dollars per mile. | Avoid assuming a model works outside its useful {domain}. A pricing rule may stop applying after a certain distance or quantity.
Fitting Linear Models to Data | A {scatter plot} displays pairs of observed values. Look for a roughly straight pattern before fitting a linear model. | A {residual} is an observed output minus the model's predicted output. Residuals show where a fitted line misses the data. | Predicting beyond the observed input range is {extrapolation}. It is less secure because the relationship may change outside the measured range.
Quadratic Functions | A quadratic function's graph is a {parabola}. For f(x) = ax² + bx + c, a must not be zero. | The {vertex} is the turning point. A positive a gives a minimum; a negative a gives a maximum. | The {zeros} are inputs where the output is zero. They appear as x-intercepts when they are real numbers.
Zeros of Polynomial Functions | A {zero} of a polynomial is an input that makes its value zero. It corresponds to a factor of the form x - c. | A zero's {multiplicity} tells how often its factor occurs. At real zeros with even multiplicity, a polynomial graph touches the axis without crossing. | The {remainder theorem} says dividing a polynomial P(x) by x - c leaves remainder P(c). A zero therefore gives remainder zero.
Modeling Using Variation | In {direct variation}, y = kx for a constant k. Doubling x doubles y when the model applies. | In {inverse variation}, y = k/x. Doubling a nonzero x halves y when k is fixed. | A {joint variation} model combines inputs, such as z = kxy. State which quantities stay fixed before predicting a change.
Exponential Functions | An {exponential function} has a variable in the exponent, such as f(x) = 3 × 2ˣ. Equal input steps multiply output by a constant factor. | In exponential {growth}, the base is greater than one. For 3 × 2ˣ, each one-unit increase in x doubles the output. | In exponential {decay}, the base is between zero and one. Multiplying by 0.8 each step retains 80% of the previous amount.
Logarithmic Properties | The {product rule} for logarithms turns multiplication into addition: log(ab) = log(a) + log(b), for positive a and b and the same valid base. | The {quotient rule} turns division into subtraction: log(a/b) = log(a) - log(b), with positive arguments and the same valid base. | The {power rule} brings down an exponent: log(aʳ) = r log(a) for positive a. It does not allow splitting log(a + b).
Fitting Exponential Models to Data | An {exponential model} fits changes that are approximately proportional to the current amount, rather than changes by a fixed amount each step. | Compare successive {ratios} for equal input steps. Roughly constant ratios suggest an exponential pattern, while constant differences suggest a linear pattern. | Check {residuals} after fitting. A convenient formula is not automatically a good model of the observed data.
Systems of Linear Equations: Two Variables | A {solution} of a two-equation system must satisfy both equations. Geometrically, it is a point shared by both lines. | In {substitution}, solve one equation for a variable, then replace that variable in the other equation with the resulting expression. | In {elimination}, add suitable multiples of the equations to cancel a variable. Then solve for the remaining variable and check both original equations.
Matrices and Matrix Operations | A {matrix} is a rectangular arrangement of numbers. Its dimensions list the number of rows first, then columns. | Add matrices {entry by entry} only when their dimensions match. A 2-by-3 matrix cannot be added to a 3-by-2 matrix. | For matrix {multiplication}, the first matrix's column count must match the second matrix's row count. The order generally matters.
Solving Systems with Cramer's Rule | A {determinant} assigns a number to a square matrix. For a 2-by-2 matrix with rows (a, b) and (c, d), it is ad - bc. | {Cramer's rule} finds each variable by a ratio of determinants, replacing that variable's coefficient column with the constants. | The rule requires a {nonzero} coefficient determinant. A zero determinant means this formula cannot determine a unique solution.
Complex Numbers | The {imaginary unit} i is defined by i² = -1. It lets us describe square roots of negative numbers. | A {complex number} has the form a + bi, with real a and b. Real numbers are included when b = 0. | A complex number's {conjugate} changes the sign of its imaginary part. The conjugate of 3 + 2i is 3 - 2i.
Angles | One full turn is {360} degrees. A right angle is one quarter of a turn, or 90 degrees. | One full turn is also {2π} radians. Radian measure connects an angle with arc length divided by radius. | {Coterminal} angles end at the same position after whole turns are added or subtracted. For example, 30 degrees and 390 degrees are coterminal.
The Other Trigonometric Functions | The {tangent} of an angle is sine divided by cosine, wherever cosine is nonzero. | The {secant} of an angle is the reciprocal of cosine. It is undefined wherever cosine equals zero. | The {cosecant} of an angle is the reciprocal of sine. Cotangent is cosine divided by sine, wherever sine is nonzero.
Right Triangle Trigonometry | In a right triangle, {sine} of an acute angle is opposite side divided by hypotenuse. Identify sides relative to the chosen angle. | In a right triangle, {cosine} of an acute angle is adjacent side divided by hypotenuse. The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle. | In a right triangle, {tangent} of an acute angle is opposite side divided by adjacent side. Similar triangles explain why these ratios depend on the angle.
Simplifying and Verifying Trigonometric Identities | An {identity} holds for every input where both sides are defined. Verifying one angle is not enough to establish it. | The {Pythagorean identity} sin²x + cos²x = 1 follows from the unit circle. Rearranging it gives useful equivalent forms. | When verifying an identity, transform one side using known identities and algebra. Keep track of the {domain}, especially before canceling factors.
Sum-to-Product and Product-to-Sum Formulas | {Product-to-sum} formulas replace products of sine and cosine with sums. They are useful when expressions are easier to combine or integrate as sums. | {Sum-to-product} formulas work in the other direction, turning sums into products using half-sums and half-differences of the angles. | Check the {signs} carefully: cos(A + B) and cos(A - B) differ in the sign of the sine product. Similar-looking formulas are not interchangeable.
Modeling with Trigonometric Functions | A {sinusoidal} model describes a repeating, wave-like pattern using sine or cosine. Examples include idealized seasonal or rotational changes. | The {amplitude} is the distance from the midline to a peak. It is half the difference between maximum and minimum values. | The {period} is the input interval for one complete cycle. In A sin(Bx) + D, it is 2π divided by the absolute value of B.
Review of Functions | A {function} assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its notation names the rule, not a multiplication. | The {domain} is the allowed input set. Check for division by zero and even roots of negative numbers in real-valued functions. | A graph's {range} is the set of outputs it reaches. It can differ from the set of possible inputs.
Trigonometric Functions | The {unit circle} has radius one and is centered at the origin. A point at angle θ has coordinates (cos θ, sin θ). | The {period} is the interval over which a function repeats. Sine and cosine repeat every 2π radians; tangent repeats every π. | The {tangent} function equals sine divided by cosine. It is undefined at angles where cosine is zero.
Exponential and Logarithmic Functions | An {exponential} function raises a fixed positive base, other than one, to a variable power. Its rate of change depends on its current value. | A {logarithm} reverses exponentiation. Saying log base b of x equals y means b raised to y equals x. | The constant {e} is the base of natural logarithms. The natural exponential function and the natural logarithm are inverse functions.
A Preview of Calculus | {Differential calculus} studies instantaneous rates of change, such as velocity at one moment rather than average speed over a trip. | {Integral calculus} studies accumulation, such as total displacement from velocity over a time interval. Signed accumulation can differ from total distance. | A {limit} describes the value a function approaches as its input approaches a point. It helps define both derivatives and integrals precisely.
The Limit Laws | The limit of a {sum} is the sum of the limits when both individual limits exist as finite numbers. | The limit of a {product} is the product of the limits when both individual limits exist as finite numbers. | For a {quotient}, divide the limits only when the denominator's limit is nonzero. An indeterminate form requires further work.
The Precise Definition of a Limit | The symbol {epsilon} represents an allowed error in the output. It measures how close f(x) should be to the proposed limit. | The symbol {delta} represents a sufficiently small distance in the input. It controls how close x must be to the target point. | A limit statement requires a suitable input distance for {every} positive output tolerance. The condition excludes x equal to the target itself.
Defining the Derivative | A {difference quotient} gives an average rate of change: [f(x + h) - f(x)]/h for nonzero h. | A {derivative} is the limit of the difference quotient as h approaches zero, when that limit exists. It gives instantaneous rate of change. | The derivative gives the slope of a {tangent line} to a differentiable curve. A sharp corner can prevent that slope from being defined.
Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions | With angles measured in radians, the derivative of sine is {cosine}. The choice of angle units matters in differentiation formulas. | With angles measured in radians, the derivative of cosine is {negative sine}. The negative sign must not be dropped. | For a composite function such as sin(x²), use the {chain rule}. Its derivative is cos(x²) times 2x.
Derivatives of Exponential and Logarithmic Functions | The derivative of eˣ is {eˣ} itself. For e raised to a function, also multiply by the derivative of that inner function. | For x > 0, the derivative of ln(x) is {1/x}. The domain restriction still matters. | For a positive base a other than one, differentiating aˣ introduces a factor of {ln(a)}. Thus its derivative is aˣ ln(a).
Related Rates | In a {related rates} problem, two or more changing quantities are connected by an equation. Their rates are linked through that equation. | Differentiate with respect to {time} before substituting a particular moment's values. Use the chain rule for quantities that depend on time. | Track the {units} and signs of rates. A shrinking radius has a negative rate if increasing radius is defined as positive.
Limits at Infinity and Asymptotes | A limit at {infinity} describes what happens as the input grows without bound. Infinity is not a number to substitute. | A {horizontal asymptote} is a line y = L approached as x tends to positive or negative infinity. A graph can cross it. | For rational functions, compare the {degrees} of numerator and denominator to determine their large-input behavior. Leading terms dominate that behavior.
Antiderivatives | An {antiderivative} of f is a function F whose derivative is f. For example, x³/3 is an antiderivative of x². | The constant {C} represents all possible vertical shifts. Since the derivative of any constant is zero, x³/3 + C has derivative x². | To {check} an antiderivative, differentiate it. The result should recover the original function on the interval being considered.
Approximating Areas | A {Riemann sum} divides an interval into subintervals and adds rectangle areas to approximate signed area under a graph. | Each rectangle's {height} is a function value at a selected point; its width is the subinterval's length. | A {definite integral} is the limit of suitable sums as the partition gets finer, when that limit exists. Regions below the axis contribute negatively.
Integration Formulas and the Net Change Theorem | An integral of a rate gives {net change} in the original quantity. For velocity, this is displacement, not necessarily total distance. | The {fundamental theorem of calculus} connects integration to antiderivatives. With a suitable antiderivative F, the integral from a to b is F(b) - F(a). | To find {total distance}, integrate speed, the absolute value of velocity. Split the interval where velocity changes sign if needed.
Integrals Resulting in Inverse Trigonometric Functions | The derivative of {arctangent} x is 1/(1 + x²). This pattern can identify an antiderivative. | The derivative of {arcsine} x is 1/√(1 - x²) for -1 < x < 1. The domain restriction is essential. | A {substitution} can turn a scaled expression into a standard inverse-trigonometric pattern. Account for the scale factor instead of matching appearances alone.
