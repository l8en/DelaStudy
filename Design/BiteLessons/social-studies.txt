What is Government? | {Government} is how a society organizes public authority and makes collective decisions. It can provide shared services and enforce laws. | A {public good} is difficult to withhold from nonpayers and can be used by one person without reducing others' use. National defense is an example. | A {democracy} gives people a role in governing, directly or through representatives. Democratic participation involves more than only voting.
Who Governs? Elitism, Pluralism, and Tradeoffs | {Elitism} argues that a relatively small group with substantial resources or influence has disproportionate power over public decisions. | {Pluralism} argues that competing groups can influence government. No single group necessarily controls every issue or decision. | A political {trade-off} means gaining something while giving up something else. Policymakers often balance competing goals and unequal pressures.
Engagement in a Democracy | {Civic engagement} includes activities intended to influence public life, such as voting, attending meetings, organizing, and contacting officials. | A {representative} acts on behalf of constituents. Citizens can evaluate representatives and communicate their priorities between elections. | {Political participation} requires opportunities as well as motivation. Time, resources, eligibility, and institutional barriers can affect who takes part.
The Pre-Revolutionary Period and the Roots of the American Political Tradition | {Natural rights} are rights people are understood to possess independently of government. This idea influenced arguments for American independence. | A {social contract} describes government as resting on an agreement between people and political authority, rather than an unlimited divine entitlement. | {Limited government} means public power is restricted by rules. Colonial experiences and English political traditions shaped this expectation, though rights were applied unequally.
The Development of the Constitution | The {Articles of Confederation} created a national government with limited powers and major difficulties raising revenue and coordinating state action. | The Constitution established {separation of powers}, dividing national responsibilities among legislative, executive, and judicial branches. | {Checks and balances} give branches ways to limit one another. A presidential veto and congressional override are examples.
Constitutional Change | An {amendment} formally changes the Constitution. Article V sets demanding proposal and ratification requirements. | {Ratification} is formal approval. A proposed federal constitutional amendment normally needs approval from three-fourths of the states. | {Judicial interpretation} can change how constitutional language applies without changing the text. Formal amendments and interpretation are different processes.
The Division of Powers | {Federalism} divides authority between national and state governments. Each level has powers rather than all authority belonging to one central government. | {Enumerated powers} are powers explicitly listed in the Constitution. Congress's authority to regulate interstate commerce is one example. | {Concurrent powers} are shared by levels of government. Taxation is one example, though each government's authority has limits.
Intergovernmental Relationships | A federal {grant} transfers money to state or local governments. Conditions can influence how the receiving government uses it. | A {categorical grant} supports a defined purpose and commonly includes detailed conditions. A block grant generally allows broader discretion within a policy area. | A federal {mandate} requires action by another level of government. Requirements and funding do not always come together.
Advantages and Disadvantages of Federalism | {Policy experimentation} lets states try different approaches. Other governments can learn from the results, though residents may experience different protections. | {Local responsiveness} can make policy better suited to a community's circumstances. It can also create uneven services across places. | {Coordination} can be difficult when several levels share responsibility. Overlapping authority can make accountability harder to follow.
What Are Civil Liberties? | {Civil liberties} protect freedoms against government interference. Examples include freedom of speech and protections in criminal proceedings. | The {Bill of Rights} contains the first ten amendments to the Constitution. Its protections have legal limits and must be interpreted. | {Selective incorporation} applies many Bill of Rights protections to states through the Fourteenth Amendment. It developed through court decisions over time.
The Rights of Suspects | {Due process} requires government to follow fair legal procedures. It protects people accused of crimes as well as those already convicted. | The Fourth Amendment limits unreasonable {searches and seizures}. Whether a warrant is required depends on the situation and recognized legal exceptions. | The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled {self-incrimination}. Accusation alone does not remove a person's constitutional rights.
Interpreting the Bill of Rights | {Freedom of speech} protects many forms of expression against government restriction. It does not mean every form of expression is protected in every setting. | The {establishment clause} and free exercise clause address government's relationship with religion. Courts interpret how these protections apply in particular cases. | A constitutional {right} can require balancing against other rights and public interests. Legal reasoning depends on facts and applicable standards, not slogans alone.
What Are Civil Rights and How Do We Identify Them? | {Civil rights} concern equal treatment and protection against discrimination. They differ from civil liberties, which focus on limits on government interference. | {Equal protection} is a central Fourteenth Amendment principle. Courts examine challenged classifications using different levels of scrutiny. | {Discrimination} means treating people differently based on a characteristic. Determining whether it violates law requires the relevant legal context.
The Fight for Women’s Rights | {Suffrage} means the right to vote. The Nineteenth Amendment prohibited denying that right on account of sex. | {Barriers} such as discriminatory state practices continued to limit many women's access to voting after the amendment. Formal rights and actual access are different. | {Equal opportunity} campaigns have addressed education, employment, political participation, and other areas. The movement did not end with voting rights.
Equal Protection for Other Groups | {Civil rights movements} have challenged exclusion affecting many groups, including people with disabilities and racial, ethnic, and religious minorities. | {Accessibility} removes barriers that prevent people from participating. Equal treatment can require changing an environment, not simply offering everyone identical conditions. | A {legal protection} can come from constitutional interpretation or legislation. Its scope depends on the particular protection and circumstances.
The Nature of Public Opinion | {Public opinion} describes people's views on political issues and institutions. It can differ across groups and change over time. | A {representative sample} aims to reflect the population being studied. A very large but biased sample can still give misleading results. | {Question wording} can affect poll responses. Check how a question was asked before interpreting its percentages.
Voter Registration | {Voter registration} records eligible voters before they vote in systems that require it. Procedures and deadlines depend on jurisdiction. | {Eligibility} is distinct from registration. Being old enough does not by itself mean every required condition or registration step has been met. | An {official election office} is the appropriate source for current voting rules. This lesson explains concepts rather than providing current registration instructions.
Direct Democracy | An {initiative} lets voters propose a law or constitutional change where that process is authorized. Requirements vary among jurisdictions. | A {referendum} puts a policy question before voters. It is a direct vote on an issue rather than the election of a representative. | A {recall} process can allow voters to remove an elected official before the term ends. Not every jurisdiction permits it.
The Institutional Design of Congress | Congress is {bicameral}: it has two chambers, the House of Representatives and the Senate. Both participate in federal lawmaking. | House seats are distributed by state {population}, while every state has two senators. The chambers therefore represent states differently. | A congressional {committee} specializes in an area of policy. Committees examine proposals, hold hearings, and help shape legislation.
The Design and Evolution of the Presidency | The {executive branch} carries out federal laws. The president leads it but does not personally perform every administrative task. | The {Electoral College} is the constitutional system for selecting the president. Winning the national popular vote alone does not determine the result. | Presidential {power} comes from the Constitution, statutes, and institutional practice. It is constrained by Congress, courts, elections, and other limits.
Presidential Governance: Direct Presidential Action | An {executive order} directs executive branch activity. It must rest on lawful authority and cannot simply replace the Constitution or a statute. | A presidential {veto} rejects a bill presented by Congress. Congress can override it with the required supermajorities in both chambers. | An {executive agreement} is an international agreement made through executive authority. It differs from a treaty requiring Senate consent.
Guardians of the Constitution and Individual Rights | {Judicial review} is the power to determine whether government actions comply with the Constitution. It can limit actions by other branches. | A {precedent} is an earlier decision used to guide later cases. Courts consider how closely the facts and legal issues match. | Federal judges' {independence} is supported by constitutional protections such as life tenure during good behavior. Independence does not mean unlimited authority.
What Is Public Policy? | {Public policy} is a course of government action or inaction addressing a public issue. It includes rules, spending choices, and implementation. | A {policy agenda} is the set of problems receiving serious public attention. Getting attention is different from securing an adopted policy. | {Implementation} turns a policy decision into practice. Resources, administrative choices, and public response affect whether it achieves its goals.
Budgeting and Tax Policy | A government {budget} sets out expected revenue and planned spending over a period. It expresses priorities as well as accounting choices. | A {deficit} occurs when spending exceeds revenue during a period. Debt is the accumulated amount owed, not the same as one year's deficit. | A {progressive tax} takes a larger share of income as income rises. Compare rates and the tax base before judging who bears a tax.
Portuguese Exploration and Spanish Conquest | European Atlantic expansion connected societies through trade, conquest, and forced movement. This growing interconnection is part of {globalization}, not a story of previously empty lands. | {Colonization} imposed outside rule on inhabited territories. Indigenous peoples responded through resistance, negotiation, and other strategies. | The {Columbian Exchange} moved plants, animals, diseases, and people across the Atlantic. Its consequences were unequal and often devastating for Indigenous communities.
Spanish Exploration and Colonial Society | Spanish {colonization} combined territorial control, extraction of resources, and religious missions. Indigenous communities faced coercion as well as cultural change. | The {encomienda} granted colonists claims to Indigenous labor and tribute under colonial authority. Its stated protective duties did not prevent severe exploitation. | A colonial {hierarchy} ranked people through ancestry, legal status, and power. Such categories supported unequal rights and access to resources.
The Impact of Colonization | {Disease} introduced through Atlantic contact caused enormous Indigenous population losses. Conquest, displacement, and forced labor compounded the damage. | {Enslavement} and forced labor supported colonial production and trade. People subjected to these systems resisted in many ways. | The {Columbian Exchange} changed diets and environments across continents. New connections brought both opportunities for some groups and catastrophic harm to others.
Confronting the National Debt: The Aftermath of the French and Indian War | The {French and Indian War} left Britain with major debt. British leaders sought more revenue from the North American colonies. | The {Stamp Act} taxed many printed materials in the colonies. Resistance linked taxation to arguments about political representation. | {Boycotts} withheld purchases to exert economic pressure. Colonists used them alongside petitions and protests against imperial policy.
Disaffection: The First Continental Congress and American Identity | The {First Continental Congress} brought colonial delegates together in 1774 to respond to British policies and coordinate resistance. | The {Continental Association} organized a colonial trade boycott. Local enforcement helped turn political opposition into collective action. | A shared {American identity} developed unevenly. Colonists disagreed about resistance, loyalty, independence, and whose rights the movement would protect.
Identity during the American Revolution | {Patriots} supported the revolutionary cause, while Loyalists remained loyal to Britain. Many people faced difficult or shifting choices. | {Republicanism} emphasized government serving the public rather than a hereditary monarch. Revolutionary ideals did not immediately produce equal political rights. | {Contradictions} shaped the Revolution: claims of liberty coexisted with slavery, Indigenous dispossession, and restrictions on women's political participation.
Common Sense: From Monarchy to an American Republic | Thomas Paine's {Common Sense} argued for independence and against monarchy in language intended to reach a broad colonial audience. | A {republic} locates political authority in the people and their representatives rather than in hereditary monarchy. Early American versions limited participation. | {Popular sovereignty} means political power ultimately rests with the people. Debates continued over who counted as part of that political community.
Debating Democracy | The {Articles of Confederation} gave the central government limited authority. Financial and political difficulties fueled calls for change. | {Shays' Rebellion} exposed conflicts over debt and taxation in Massachusetts. Some leaders used it to argue for a stronger national government. | {Democracy} was contested after independence. Americans disagreed about representation, property requirements, and how much direct influence ordinary voters should have.
The Constitutional Convention and Federal Constitution | The {Constitutional Convention} met in Philadelphia in 1787. Delegates ultimately proposed a new federal system rather than merely revising the Articles. | The {Great Compromise} created a population-based House and equal state representation in the Senate. It settled one major dispute over representation. | The {Three-Fifths Compromise} counted enslaved people for representation and taxation without granting them rights. It increased slaveholding states' political power.
Lewis and Clark | The {Louisiana Purchase} greatly expanded United States territorial claims in 1803. Indigenous nations already occupied and governed these lands. | The {Lewis and Clark expedition} mapped routes and gathered information while relying on Indigenous knowledge, trade, and assistance. | {Sacagawea}, a Shoshone woman, contributed interpretation and regional knowledge to the expedition. Expansion also intensified pressure on Indigenous communities.
Free or Slave Soil? The Dilemma of the West | {Westward expansion} intensified disputes over whether new territories would permit slavery. These disputes linked land, political power, and forced labor. | {Popular sovereignty} proposed letting territorial settlers decide the slavery question. In practice, it did not resolve conflict peacefully. | {Bleeding Kansas} refers to violence over slavery in Kansas Territory. It showed how territorial politics deepened the national crisis.
John Brown and the Election of 1860 | {John Brown} led an 1859 raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, hoping to spark resistance to slavery. The raid failed. | The election of {1860} exposed deep sectional divisions. Abraham Lincoln won the presidency without winning a Southern state. | {Secession} was the withdrawal of Southern states from the Union. Protecting slavery was central to the secession crisis.
Restoring the Union | {Reconstruction} concerned the return of former Confederate states and the meaning of freedom after the Civil War. | The {Thirteenth Amendment} abolished slavery except as punishment for crime. Ending legal slavery did not by itself establish equal citizenship or safety. | {Black Codes} restricted the freedom and labor of newly emancipated people. They showed how former Confederate states sought to preserve racial control.
Radical Reconstruction, 1867–1872 | {Congressional Reconstruction} imposed conditions for former Confederate states' return to representation and expanded federal protection of civil rights. | The {Fourteenth Amendment} established national birthright citizenship and equal protection principles. Its enforcement became a continuing political struggle. | The {Fifteenth Amendment} prohibited denying voting rights on account of race, color, or previous servitude. Violence and other barriers still obstructed voting.
The Collapse of Reconstruction | {White supremacist violence} targeted Black citizens and their allies, undermining voting, officeholding, and civil rights during Reconstruction. | {Federal retreat} weakened enforcement of Reconstruction protections. Political compromise and changing national priorities helped end sustained intervention. | {Disenfranchisement} later stripped many Black citizens of effective voting access through laws, intimidation, and discriminatory practices despite constitutional amendments.
Developing a Global Perspective | A {global perspective} examines connections among societies rather than treating each nation's history as separate and self-contained. | {Chronology} places events in time order. It helps establish what happened before or after an event but does not by itself prove causation. | {Context} means the surrounding conditions that give an event meaning. Historical judgments need the circumstances and perspectives of the people involved.
Primary Sources | A {primary source} comes from the time or people being studied. Letters, objects, images, and oral accounts can all serve as evidence. | {Sourcing} asks who made a source, when, for whom, and why. A source's purpose affects what it reveals and omits. | {Corroboration} compares independent evidence. Agreement can strengthen an interpretation, while disagreement can reveal differing experiences or interests.
Causation and Interpretation in History | A historical {cause} helps explain why an event occurred. Events often have several interacting causes rather than one simple trigger. | An {interpretation} is an evidence-based explanation of the past. Different interpretations should be evaluated by their evidence and reasoning. | {Correlation} means events occur together or vary together. It does not establish that one caused the other.
India and International Connections | The {Indian Ocean} connected South Asian ports with trading partners in Africa, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia. | Seasonal {monsoon winds} shaped sailing routes and travel schedules. Knowledge of these winds helped sustain long-distance maritime trade. | {Cultural exchange} accompanied commerce. Travelers and merchants carried religious ideas, languages, and practices as well as goods.
The Malacca Sultanate | {Malacca} became an important port near a narrow passage linking the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Its location supported trade. | A {sultanate} is a state ruled by a sultan. Malacca linked political authority with a diverse maritime trading community. | Merchant {networks} connected Malacca to distant markets. Trade also supported the spread of Islam through parts of Southeast Asia.
Exchange in East Asia | East Asian {trade networks} connected ports, states, and communities through the movement of goods, people, and ideas. | The {tributary system} organized some diplomatic and trading relationships around recognition of a Chinese ruler's status. Actual relationships varied. | {Maritime exchange} depended on political decisions as well as ships and markets. Restrictions could redirect trade without eliminating demand for foreign goods.
The Roots of African Trade | African {trade networks} connected diverse communities long before European Atlantic expansion. They exchanged goods, knowledge, and cultural practices. | {Gold} from parts of West Africa helped connect regional economies with North Africa and wider markets. | {Caravan routes} crossed the Sahara using knowledge of water sources and desert travel. They linked trade centers rather than erasing the region's difficulties.
The Swahili Coast | The {Swahili Coast} linked East African communities to Indian Ocean trade. Port cities connected inland resources with overseas markets. | {Kiswahili} is a Bantu language with vocabulary influenced by contact with Arabic and other languages. It reflects regional interaction rather than a single outside origin. | {City-states} such as Kilwa developed around maritime commerce. Their wealth and political relationships changed as trade routes and outside pressures changed.
The Trans-Saharan Slave Trade | The {trans-Saharan trade} included the forced movement of enslaved people along routes connecting sub-Saharan Africa and North Africa. | {Enslavement} took different forms across societies, but it involved severe coercion and loss of freedom. Differences do not remove that harm. | Historical {sources} on slavery must be read critically. Records by traders or rulers can obscure the experiences and resistance of enslaved people.
A Connected Islamic World | {Islamic networks} connected regions through scholarship, pilgrimage, trade, and political ties. Muslim societies remained culturally and politically diverse. | The {hajj} is the pilgrimage to Mecca. Travel for pilgrimage also supported encounters among people from distant regions. | {Scholarly exchange} carried ideas across languages and borders. Shared religion did not mean every state or community followed identical practices.
The Ottoman Empire | The {Ottoman Empire} developed from a regional state into a large empire spanning parts of Europe, Asia, and Africa. | {Constantinople} was conquered by the Ottomans in 1453 and became an imperial capital. Its location connected important land and sea routes. | Ottoman {administration} governed diverse populations through institutions, local arrangements, and imperial authority. Diversity did not imply equal legal status for every group.
The Safavid Empire | The {Safavid Empire} established a major state in Iran in the early sixteenth century. It shaped the region's political and religious development. | {Shia Islam} became central to Safavid state identity. This distinguished the dynasty from neighboring Sunni-ruled powers. | Safavid-Ottoman {rivalry} involved territory, political authority, and religious differences. It cannot be explained by religion alone.
The Enlightenment | The {Enlightenment} emphasized reason, inquiry, and criticism of inherited authority. Its thinkers disagreed on many political and social questions. | {Natural rights} arguments challenged the idea that rulers could exercise unlimited power. These ideas influenced later revolutionary movements. | Enlightenment {contradictions} included universal claims about liberty alongside slavery, empire, and women's exclusion. Study who was included as well as what was declared.
Revolutions: America, France, and Haiti | The {American Revolution} challenged British rule and created an independent republic, while leaving slavery and many exclusions in place. | The {French Revolution} challenged monarchy and privilege. Its changing governments and conflicts show that revolution was a process, not one event. | The {Haitian Revolution} overthrew slavery and colonial rule in Saint-Domingue. Haiti declared independence in 1804 after a long struggle.
Nationalism, Liberalism, Conservatism, and the Political Order | {Nationalism} links political identity to a nation or people. It can support independence movements or efforts to exclude those defined as outsiders. | Nineteenth-century {liberalism} often favored constitutional government, civil liberties, and limits on inherited privilege, though participation was frequently restricted. | {Conservatism} sought to preserve established institutions or restore order after revolution. Its goals varied with local traditions and political circumstances.
