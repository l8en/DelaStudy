# Short, selected explanations adapted from the named OpenStax sections.
The Science of Biology | {Biology} is the study of life. Biologists study living things at scales ranging from molecules to whole ecosystems. | A {hypothesis} is a testable explanation for an observation. A useful test could produce evidence against it, not just evidence in its favor. | A {controlled experiment} changes a chosen factor while keeping other relevant conditions comparable. This helps separate the factor's effect from other explanations.
Atoms, Isotopes, Ions, and Molecules: The Building Blocks | An atom's {protons} determine its element. Every carbon atom has six protons, even if its number of neutrons changes. | {Isotopes} are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number but different masses. | An {ion} is an atom or group of atoms with a net electric charge because electrons have been gained or lost.
Carbon | {Carbon} can form four covalent bonds, allowing it to build chains, branches, and rings in biological molecules. | {Isomers} have the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms. Their shapes can give them different properties. | A {functional group} is a group of atoms that helps give an organic molecule its chemical behavior. Hydroxyl groups are one example.
Synthesis of Biological Macromolecules | A {monomer} is a small building block. Linking many monomers can create a large molecule called a polymer. | {Dehydration synthesis} joins building blocks while releasing water. Cells use enzyme-controlled reactions to build many large biological molecules. | {Hydrolysis} breaks a bond by adding water. It helps cells split large molecules into smaller parts.
Lipids | {Lipids} are a varied group of molecules that generally mix poorly with water. Fats, phospholipids, and steroids belong to this group. | A {triglyceride} contains glycerol joined to three fatty acids. It is an important form of long-term energy storage. | A {phospholipid} has a water-attracting head and water-avoiding tails. This combination helps phospholipids form the two-layer structure of cell membranes.
Nucleic Acids | {DNA} stores hereditary information in its base sequence. Its two strands pair through A with T and C with G. | A {nucleotide} contains a sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen-containing base. Nucleotides are the building blocks of DNA and RNA. | {RNA} usually contains ribose sugar and uses uracil instead of thymine. Different types help cells use genetic information.
Studying Cells | A {microscope} makes structures too small to see unaided visible. Different microscope types reveal different details. | {Magnification} makes an image look larger. It does not necessarily reveal extra detail if the original image is blurry. | {Resolution} is the ability to distinguish nearby objects as separate. Better resolution, not just higher magnification, reveals smaller cell structures.
The Endomembrane System and Proteins | The {rough endoplasmic reticulum} has attached ribosomes that make many proteins destined for membranes or secretion. It begins their processing. | The {Golgi apparatus} modifies, sorts, and packages proteins and lipids. Small transport vesicles move materials between compartments. | A {lysosome} contains enzymes that break down materials in animal cells. Its acidic interior helps those enzymes work.
Connections between Cells and Cellular Activities | The {extracellular matrix} surrounds many animal cells. It provides support and also helps cells receive information from their surroundings. | {Tight junctions} help seal neighboring animal cells together, limiting movement of material between them. | {Gap junctions} connect neighboring animal cells through channels. Small molecules and ions can pass through them to coordinate activity.
Components and Structure | A cell membrane's {phospholipid bilayer} has water-attracting heads facing outward and water-avoiding tails facing inward. | Membrane {proteins} perform jobs such as transport, signaling, and attachment. The membrane is more than a passive wall. | {Selective permeability} means some substances cross a membrane more easily than others. Size, charge, and chemical properties affect passage.
Active Transport | {Active transport} moves substances against their concentration or electrochemical gradient. It requires an energy source rather than relying on diffusion alone. | The {sodium-potassium pump} uses ATP to move sodium out of a cell and potassium into it. This helps maintain ion gradients. | {Secondary active transport} uses energy stored in an ion gradient to move another substance. ATP may power the gradient rather than the transport step directly.
Bulk Transport | {Endocytosis} brings material into a cell by forming a membrane-bound vesicle. The membrane bends around the material. | {Exocytosis} releases material when a vesicle joins the cell membrane. Cells use it to secrete substances such as some hormones. | {Receptor-mediated endocytosis} selects particular cargo using membrane receptors. This is more specific than simply taking in nearby fluid.
Energy and Metabolism | {Metabolism} is the sum of a cell's chemical reactions. Some reactions break molecules down; others build them up. | An {enzyme} speeds a reaction by lowering its activation energy. It does not change the reaction's overall energy difference. | {ATP} transfers usable energy between cellular reactions. Breaking down nutrients can supply energy to regenerate ATP from ADP and phosphate.
Glycolysis | {Glycolysis} splits one glucose into two pyruvate molecules. It takes place in the cytoplasm and does not directly require oxygen. | Each glucose yields a net gain of {two ATP} during glycolysis. Some ATP is spent before more is produced. | {NADH} carries high-energy electrons removed during glycolysis. Later reactions must regenerate NAD+ so glycolysis can continue.
Regulation of Cellular Respiration | {Feedback inhibition} occurs when a pathway's products slow an earlier step. This helps prevent cells from making more than they need. | {Phosphofructokinase} controls an important step in glycolysis. Its activity responds to signals about the cell's energy supply. | High {ATP} availability can slow parts of respiration. Cells adjust energy production instead of running every reaction at maximum speed continuously.
Carbohydrates | {Carbohydrates} include sugars and their larger assemblies. Cells use them for fuel, energy storage, and structural support. | {Glucose} is a six-carbon sugar used in cellular respiration. Plants can link glucose units into starch for storage. | {Cellulose} is a structural carbohydrate in plant cell walls. Its bonds differ from those in starch, so humans cannot digest it using their own enzymes.
Oxidation of Pyruvate and the Citric Acid Cycle | Pyruvate is converted to {acetyl CoA} before entering the citric acid cycle. This conversion releases carbon dioxide and transfers electrons to NAD+. | The {citric acid cycle} processes acetyl groups through a series of reactions. It releases carbon dioxide and regenerates its starting acceptor molecule. | {Electron carriers} such as NADH and FADH2 deliver electrons to later respiration stages. Most ATP from aerobic respiration is made after the cycle itself.
Using Light Energy to Make Organic Molecules | The {Calvin cycle} uses ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to help build sugar precursors from carbon dioxide. | {Carbon fixation} attaches carbon dioxide to an organic molecule. The enzyme RuBisCO carries out this entry step in the Calvin cycle. | The product {G3P} is a three-carbon molecule. Some leaves the cycle to help build carbohydrates; the rest helps regenerate the carbon dioxide acceptor.
Cell Division | A {chromosome} is a packaged DNA molecule with associated proteins. Cells must copy and distribute chromosomes before completing division. | {Mitosis} separates duplicated chromosomes into two nuclei. DNA replication occurs earlier, during the S phase of interphase. | {Cytokinesis} divides the cell's cytoplasm. It is distinct from nuclear division, even though the two processes are closely coordinated.
Prokaryotic Cell Division | Most prokaryotes reproduce by {binary fission}. A cell copies its DNA, separates the copies, and divides into two cells. | A bacterial chromosome begins copying at an {origin of replication}. Replication must finish so each new cell receives genetic information. | A dividing bacterial cell forms a {septum}, a partition that helps separate the daughter cells. Bacteria do not use mitosis to divide.
Laws of Inheritance | Different versions of a gene are called {alleles}. A diploid organism generally has two alleles for each autosomal gene. | The law of {segregation} says the two alleles separate during gamete formation. Each gamete receives one allele from that pair. | {Independent assortment} describes how alleles of unlinked genes can be distributed independently. Closely linked genes are an important exception.
Historical Basis of Modern Understanding | Experiments with bacterial {transformation} showed that cells could take up material that changed inherited traits. Researchers then worked to identify that material. | Evidence from several experiments identified {DNA} as genetic material. Scientific conclusions developed through multiple tests, not one observation alone. | The {double helix} model explained how DNA's paired strands could store information and provide templates for copying it.
DNA Repair | {DNA repair} corrects many kinds of damage and copying errors. It reduces mutation rates but does not make DNA error-free. | During {proofreading}, a DNA polymerase can remove an incorrectly added nucleotide and replace it with the correct one. | A permanent change in a DNA sequence is a {mutation}. Some have little effect; others alter cell function or inherited traits.
Ribosomes and Protein Synthesis | {Translation} uses an mRNA sequence to build a chain of amino acids. Ribosomes coordinate the process. | A {codon} is a sequence of three mRNA bases. It specifies an amino acid or a stop signal. | A {tRNA} carries an amino acid and pairs its anticodon with an mRNA codon. This connects the nucleotide message to the growing protein.
The Scope of Ecology | {Ecology} studies how organisms interact with one another and with their physical environment. It links living and nonliving parts of a system. | A {population} consists of members of one species in an area. A community includes interacting populations of different species. | An {ecosystem} includes a community and its nonliving environment. Water, soil, temperature, and energy are part of its study.
Terrestrial Biomes | A {biome} is a broad ecological region with characteristic climate and vegetation. Similar climates can support similar biomes on different continents. | {Temperature} and precipitation strongly influence which plants can grow. Their seasonal patterns matter as well as their yearly averages. | A {desert} receives little precipitation. Deserts are not always hot; low rainfall, rather than heat alone, defines them.
Climate and the Effects of Global Climate Change | {Climate} describes long-term weather patterns. A cold day does not by itself show whether the global climate is warming or cooling. | {Greenhouse gases} absorb and emit infrared radiation, warming Earth's surface and lower atmosphere. Increasing their concentrations changes Earth's energy balance. | Climate change can shift species' {ranges}, seasonal timing, and interactions. Different species may respond at different rates.
Population Demography | {Demography} studies a population's size, structure, and changes. Births, deaths, immigration, and emigration all affect population size. | {Population density} is the number of individuals per unit area or volume. It is different from the total population count. | A {life table} summarizes survival and reproduction across age groups. It can help reveal when mortality is highest.
Population Dynamics and Regulation | {Exponential growth} occurs when population change is proportional to current population size under an idealized unlimited-resource model. | {Carrying capacity} is the population an environment can support under given conditions. It can change when resources or conditions change. | {Density-dependent} factors become more influential as crowding increases. Competition and the spread of some diseases are examples.
Behavioral Biology: Proximate and Ultimate Causes of Behavior | A {proximate cause} explains how a behavior happens, such as a hormone or environmental cue that triggers it. | An {ultimate cause} considers how a behavior's evolutionary history relates to survival or reproduction. It is not a conscious purpose assigned to the animal. | {Learning} changes behavior through experience. Inherited tendencies and experience can both contribute to the same behavior.
Ecology of Ecosystems | {Biotic} components are living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, and microbes. They interact with nonliving conditions. | {Abiotic} components include sunlight, temperature, water, and minerals. Changing them can affect the entire biological community. | A {food web} shows connected feeding relationships. Unlike a single food chain, it represents multiple paths through a community.
Energy Flow through Ecosystems | {Primary producers} capture energy to build organic molecules. Most use sunlight, while some use energy from chemical reactions. | A {trophic level} groups organisms by their feeding position. Producers occupy the first level; organisms feeding on them occupy higher levels. | Energy transfer is {inefficient}: organisms use energy and release heat. Less energy is available to support organisms at higher feeding levels.
Biogeochemical Cycles | A {biogeochemical cycle} traces matter through living organisms and nonliving reservoirs. Matter cycles even though energy flows through ecosystems. | {Nitrogen fixation} converts atmospheric nitrogen into forms organisms can use. Certain microbes perform this essential step. | A {reservoir} stores a chemical element within a cycle. Oceans, rocks, the atmosphere, and organisms hold different amounts for different lengths of time.
The Biodiversity Crisis | {Biodiversity} includes variation in genes, species, and ecosystems. Counting species alone does not capture every kind of biological diversity. | An {extinction} occurs when a species disappears everywhere. Losing a species from one area is a local loss, not necessarily a global extinction. | {Habitat loss} reduces or changes the places organisms need to survive. It is a major threat to biodiversity.
Threats to Biodiversity | {Habitat fragmentation} splits a larger habitat into smaller patches. It can isolate populations and change conditions near habitat edges. | An {invasive species} spreads outside its native range and causes harm. Not every nonnative species becomes invasive. | {Overharvesting} removes organisms faster than populations can replace them. It can threaten species even when their habitat remains intact.
Preserving Biodiversity | {Protected areas} conserve habitats, but their size, location, and management affect how well they protect species. | A {wildlife corridor} connects habitat patches. It can help organisms move between otherwise isolated areas. | {Restoration ecology} aims to help damaged ecosystems recover. Recovery requires attention to physical conditions, species interactions, and ongoing threats.
The Plant Body | {Roots} anchor a plant and absorb water and minerals. Their branching and root hairs increase contact with the soil. | {Shoots} include stems, leaves, and reproductive structures. They support growth above ground and connect resources within the plant. | {Vascular tissue} transports materials. Xylem carries water and minerals; phloem transports sugars between sources and places that use or store them.
Leaves | Leaves are major sites of {photosynthesis}, where light energy helps plants make organic molecules from carbon dioxide and water. | {Stomata} are pores that let gases move into and out of leaves. Opening them also allows water vapor to escape. | {Guard cells} control stomatal openings. They help balance carbon dioxide intake against water loss.
Plant Sensory Systems and Responses | A {tropism} is directional growth in response to a stimulus. Shoots often bend toward light, while roots respond to gravity. | {Auxin} is a plant hormone involved in growth. Uneven auxin activity can contribute to bending by changing growth on opposite sides. | {Photoperiodism} is a response to the length of day or night. It helps some plants time flowering to seasonal conditions.
Chemistry in Context | {Chemistry} studies matter: what it contains, how it behaves, and how it changes. Cooking, batteries, and medicine all involve chemical processes. | A {hypothesis} is a testable explanation. Observations can support it or show that it needs to be changed. | A scientific {law} summarizes a consistent pattern in observations. A theory explains patterns; it does not become a law simply by gaining more evidence.
Measurements | A {measurement} contains a number and a unit. Stating 5 without saying grams, meters, or another unit leaves the quantity unclear. | {Accuracy} describes closeness to an accepted or reference value. It is different from how closely repeated measurements agree with one another. | {Precision} describes agreement among repeated measurements. A device can give very consistent results that are all offset from the correct value.
Mathematical Treatment of Measurement Results | {Dimensional analysis} uses units to set up calculations. Arrange conversion factors so the units you do not want cancel. | {Significant figures} communicate measurement precision. A calculated result should not imply more precision than its measured inputs support. | In {scientific notation}, a number is written as a coefficient times a power of ten. For example, 4500 can be written as 4.5 × 10³.
Early Ideas in Atomic Theory | An {atom} is the smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical identity. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms rather than changing one element into another. | The law of {definite proportions} says a pure compound contains its elements in a fixed mass ratio. Water always has the same elemental proportions. | The law of {multiple proportions} compares different compounds made from the same elements. Their combining masses form ratios of small whole numbers.
Chemical Formulas | A {chemical formula} shows which elements are present and how many atoms or what ratio of atoms a substance contains. | A {molecular formula} gives the actual number of each kind of atom in a molecule. H2O contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. | An {empirical formula} gives the simplest whole-number ratio. The molecular formula C6H12O6 has empirical formula CH2O.
Chemical Nomenclature | {Nomenclature} is the system used to name substances consistently. A compound's name should communicate information about its composition. | An {ionic compound} contains positive and negative ions. Its formula balances their charges so the whole compound is electrically neutral. | A {Roman numeral} in a metal ion's name states its charge. Iron(III) means an iron ion with a positive-three charge, not three iron atoms.
Formula Mass and the Mole Concept | A {mole} contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. State whether you are counting atoms, molecules, ions, or other particles. | {Molar mass} is the mass of one mole of a substance, commonly measured in grams per mole. | A compound's {formula mass} is the sum of the atomic masses represented by its formula. Subscripts tell how many of each atom to include.
Molarity | {Molarity} is moles of solute divided by liters of solution. A 1-molar solution has one mole of solute per liter of solution. | The {solute} is the dissolved substance. The solvent is the substance in which it dissolves. | In a simple {dilution}, adding solvent lowers concentration without changing the moles of solute. The relation M1V1 = M2V2 applies under that condition.
Other Units for Solution Concentrations | {Mass percent} is solute mass divided by total solution mass, multiplied by 100. Use the same mass units in both parts. | {Parts per million} describes a small proportion: one part solute per million parts of the mixture, using the stated measurement basis. | {Molality} is moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. Unlike molarity, its denominator does not use solution volume.
Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations | A balanced equation conserves {atoms} of every element. Count atoms on each side before deciding whether it is balanced. | Adjust {coefficients} to balance an equation. Do not change subscripts, because that would change the substances themselves. | In 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, the ratio of hydrogen molecules to oxygen molecules is {two to one}. Coefficients also give mole ratios.
Reaction Stoichiometry | {Stoichiometry} uses a balanced equation to relate quantities of reactants and products. Its coefficients give mole ratios, not mass ratios. | A {limiting reactant} runs out first and limits how much product can form. Other reactants may remain after the reaction stops. | {Theoretical yield} is the maximum product predicted from the limiting reactant. Actual yield can be lower because reactions and recovery are not perfect.
Quantitative Chemical Analysis | A {titration} uses a solution of known concentration to determine the amount of another substance through a measured reaction. | At the {equivalence point}, reactants have been combined in the mole ratio required by the balanced equation. This is not always at pH 7. | {Gravimetric analysis} determines an amount from mass, often by collecting and weighing a precipitate whose composition is known.
Electromagnetic Energy | Light's {wavelength} is the distance between successive wave peaks. Its frequency is the number of cycles passing a point per second. | A {photon} is a packet of electromagnetic energy. Its energy increases with frequency and decreases as wavelength increases. | In a vacuum, wavelength times frequency equals the {speed of light}. This links the two wave measurements.
Development of Quantum Theory | An {orbital} describes a quantum state and the spatial probability pattern for an electron. It is not a fixed planetary orbit. | Electron energies in an atom are {quantized}, meaning only certain energy values are allowed. Changes between levels involve specific energy differences. | The {uncertainty principle} limits how precisely position and momentum can be known together. It is not simply a problem with poor measuring equipment.
Periodic Variations in Element Properties | {Atomic radius} generally decreases across a period and increases down a group. Effective nuclear attraction and additional electron shells help explain the pattern. | {Ionization energy} is the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion. It generally increases across a period. | {Electronegativity} describes an atom's attraction for shared electrons in a bond. It is not the same quantity as ionization energy.
Energy Basics | {Energy} is the capacity to transfer heat or do work. Chemical changes can transfer energy between a system and its surroundings. | {Kinetic energy} is associated with motion. Faster particles generally have more kinetic energy, though mass also matters. | {Potential energy} depends on position or arrangement. Chemical bonds and interactions contribute to a substance's stored energy.
Calorimetry | {Calorimetry} measures heat transfer by observing temperature changes under known conditions. The setup must account for what absorbs or releases heat. | {Specific heat} is the heat needed to raise one unit of mass by one degree. Water's high specific heat helps it resist rapid temperature changes. | In q = mcΔT, {temperature change} is final temperature minus initial temperature. Keep the sign to show whether the material warms or cools.
Enthalpy | {Enthalpy} is a state function useful for describing heat changes at constant pressure. Its change is written ΔH. | An {exothermic} process releases heat to the surroundings and has a negative enthalpy change under the usual convention. | An {endothermic} process absorbs heat from the surroundings and has a positive enthalpy change under the usual convention.
Ionic Bonding | An {ionic bond} is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Many ionic solids form extended lattices rather than separate molecules. | A {cation} is positively charged. A metal atom can form one by losing electrons. | An {anion} is negatively charged. A nonmetal atom can form one by gaining electrons.
Molecular Structure and Polarity | {VSEPR} predicts molecular shapes by treating electron groups around a central atom as repelling one another. Lone pairs count as electron groups. | A {polar bond} shares electrons unequally because the bonded atoms attract them differently. Bond polarity alone does not determine the whole molecule's polarity. | A molecule's {geometry} determines whether bond dipoles cancel. Carbon dioxide has polar bonds but a nonpolar overall arrangement.
Molecular Orbital Theory | A {molecular orbital} describes an electron state spread across a molecule. Atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals. | A {bonding orbital} concentrates electron density in a way that stabilizes the bonded atoms. An antibonding orbital has the opposite effect. | {Bond order} is half the number of bonding electrons minus antibonding electrons. Higher bond order generally indicates a stronger, shorter bond for comparable atoms.
Physics: Definitions and Applications | {Physics} studies matter, energy, motion, and interactions. Its models connect many everyday events with the same underlying principles. | {Kinematics} describes motion without asking what causes it. Position, velocity, and acceleration are its central quantities. | {Dynamics} studies how forces affect motion. It asks why an object's velocity changes rather than only describing that change.
The Scientific Methods | An {observation} is information gathered from measurement or experience. Separate what was observed from your interpretation of it. | A {hypothesis} proposes a testable explanation. An experiment should be able to reveal whether its predictions fail. | A scientific {model} represents selected features of a system. It is useful within stated assumptions, not automatically accurate in every situation.
The Language of Physics: Physical Quantities and Units | A {physical quantity} combines a numerical value with a unit. Writing 10 meters per second communicates more than writing 10. | The SI unit of time is the {second}. SI uses consistent base units to build derived units such as meters per second. | {Dimensional analysis} checks whether an equation's units fit. An equation that adds a length to a time cannot describe a valid physical relationship.
Relative Motion, Distance, and Displacement | {Distance} is the total path length traveled. It does not include a direction and cannot be negative. | {Displacement} is the change in position from start to finish. A round trip can have positive distance but zero displacement. | A {reference frame} sets the viewpoint for measuring motion. A passenger may be at rest relative to a train but moving relative to the ground.
Position vs. Time Graphs | On a position-time graph, the {slope} gives velocity. A steeper line means a larger velocity magnitude, not a larger position alone. | A {horizontal} segment means position stays constant, so the object is at rest during that interval. | A {curved} position-time graph has a changing slope. It indicates that velocity changes rather than remaining constant.
Velocity vs. Time Graphs | On a velocity-time graph, the {slope} gives acceleration. A rising line means velocity is increasing, even if it starts negative. | The signed {area} under a velocity-time graph gives displacement. Portions below the time axis contribute negatively. | The graph crossing {zero velocity} may mark a change in direction. Check the signs on both sides, not merely the crossing itself.
Acceleration | {Acceleration} is the rate at which velocity changes. It can involve a change in speed, direction, or both. | {Average acceleration} equals change in velocity divided by elapsed time. Its SI unit is meters per second squared. | A {negative} acceleration does not always mean slowing down. Speed increases when velocity and acceleration point in the same direction.
Angle of Rotation and Angular Velocity | {Angular displacement} measures how far an object turns. Radians express this as arc length divided by radius. | {Angular velocity} is the rate of change of angular position. It differs from linear speed, which depends on distance from the rotation axis. | For circular motion, {tangential speed} equals radius times angular speed when angular speed is measured in radians per unit time.
Rotational Motion | {Angular acceleration} measures how quickly angular velocity changes. Its units are radians per second squared. | An object's {moment of inertia} describes its resistance to changes in rotation. It depends on how mass is distributed around the chosen axis. | A {torque} is a turning effect of a force. Both force and its perpendicular distance from the axis matter.
Force | A {force} is an interaction that can change an object's motion. Forces have both magnitude and direction. | The {net force} is the vector sum of all external forces on an object. Individual forces can cancel. | When net force is {zero}, velocity remains constant in an inertial frame. This includes rest but does not require rest.
Newton's Second Law of Motion | For constant mass, net force equals mass times {acceleration}: F = ma. The force and acceleration vectors point in the same direction. | {Mass} measures an object's inertia. With the same net force, a larger mass has a smaller acceleration. | One {newton} is the force that gives a one-kilogram mass an acceleration of one meter per second squared.
Newton's Third Law of Motion | Forces come in {pairs}: when object A exerts a force on B, B exerts an equal-magnitude, opposite-direction force on A. | The two forces act on {different objects}. They do not cancel in a force diagram for just one object. | A person's push on the ground and the ground's push on the person are an {interaction pair}. The ground's force can accelerate the person forward.
Linear Momentum, Force, and Impulse | {Momentum} equals mass times velocity. Because velocity has direction, momentum is a vector quantity. | {Impulse} is the change in momentum. For a constant net force, it equals force times the time interval. | Total momentum is {conserved} when a system's net external impulse is zero. Internal collision forces can be large without changing that total.
Work, Power, and the Work–Energy Theorem | {Work} is energy transferred by a force acting through displacement. Only the force component along the displacement contributes. | Net work equals the change in {kinetic energy}. A positive net work increases an object's kinetic energy. | {Power} is the rate of doing work or transferring energy. Transferring the same energy in less time requires greater average power.
Simple Machines | A {simple machine} changes how a force is applied, often trading a smaller input force for a greater input distance. | {Mechanical advantage} compares output force with input force. It does not mean the machine creates energy. | {Efficiency} compares useful output work with input work. Friction and other losses make real machines less than perfectly efficient.
The Scope and Scale of Physics | A {physical model} simplifies a system so it can be studied. Its assumptions determine where its predictions are useful. | An {order of magnitude} compares approximate size using powers of ten. It helps make sense of very large and very small scales. | {Units} connect a numerical value to a physical quantity. A number without its unit can be ambiguous or misleading.
Significant Figures | {Significant figures} communicate how precisely a measured quantity is known. Extra digits from a calculator do not automatically add measurement precision. | In multiplication and division, the least precise input usually limits the number of significant digits in the {result}. | For addition and subtraction, round according to the least precise {decimal place} among the inputs, rather than simply counting significant figures.
Products of Vectors | A {dot product} produces a scalar: A · B = AB cos θ. It measures how strongly two vectors point in the same direction. | A {cross product} produces a vector perpendicular to both input vectors. Its magnitude is AB sin θ. | Use the {right-hand rule} to find a cross product's direction. Reversing the order of the two vectors reverses that direction.
Position, Displacement, and Average Velocity | {Position} locates an object relative to an origin and coordinate system. It is not the same as how far the object has traveled. | {Displacement} is final position minus initial position. It depends only on the endpoints, not the route. | {Average velocity} equals displacement divided by elapsed time. A round trip can have zero average velocity even while the traveler is moving much of the time.
Finding Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration | Integrating {acceleration} over time gives the change in velocity. Add the initial velocity to obtain the final velocity. | Integrating {velocity} over time gives displacement. Signed areas matter when the velocity changes direction. | An {initial condition} supplies a starting value. Integration alone gives a family of possible functions until that value is included.
Relative Motion in One and Two Dimensions | A {reference frame} is the viewpoint and coordinates used to describe motion. Different observers can measure different velocities for the same object. | In classical relative motion, {velocity vectors} add. A boat's velocity relative to the water combines with the water's velocity relative to shore. | Resolve two-dimensional motion into {components}. Add horizontal components together and vertical components together before finding the resulting magnitude and direction.
Forces | A {force} describes an interaction between objects. It is a vector, so both strength and direction are needed. | The {net force} is the vector sum of external forces acting on the selected object. Do not add magnitudes without considering directions. | A {contact force} acts through contact, such as friction or tension. Gravity is an example of a force that acts without contact.
Mass and Weight | {Mass} measures inertia and is expressed in kilograms. It does not change simply because an object moves to a different planet. | {Weight} is the gravitational force on an object. Near Earth's surface, its magnitude is approximately mg. | A scale measures a supporting {normal force}, not mass directly. Its reading can change in an accelerating elevator even though mass stays the same.
Drawing Free-Body Diagrams | A {free-body diagram} shows the external forces acting on one chosen object. Leave out forces that object exerts on other things. | The {normal force} acts perpendicular to a contact surface. It is not always equal in magnitude to the object's weight. | Choose {axes} that simplify the problem, such as one parallel to a ramp. Resolve forces into components before applying Newton's second law.
Solving Problems with Newton’s Laws | First define the {system} you are analyzing. Forces crossing its boundary are external; interactions entirely inside it are internal. | Apply {Newton's second law} separately along each axis. The sum of force components equals mass times the acceleration component. | A consistent {sign convention} prevents many errors. A negative answer may indicate direction opposite to your chosen positive axis, not an impossible result.
Centripetal Force | {Centripetal acceleration} points toward the center of a circular path. Its magnitude is v²/r, even when speed is constant. | The needed inward {net force} is mv²/r for an object of mass m. It comes from real interactions such as tension, friction, or gravity. | Centripetal force is not an {extra force} added to a diagram. It describes the inward result of the actual forces already acting.
Drag Force and Terminal Speed | {Drag} opposes motion relative to a fluid such as air or water. Its magnitude depends on speed, shape, and fluid properties. | {Terminal speed} occurs when drag and other forces balance, making acceleration zero. The object may still be moving rapidly. | At terminal speed during a vertical fall with negligible buoyancy, drag balances {weight}. Zero acceleration does not mean no forces are acting.
Work | {Work} is the dot product of force and displacement for a constant force. A perpendicular force does zero work on that displacement. | {Positive work} adds kinetic energy when it is the net work on an object. Negative net work reduces kinetic energy. | For a changing force along a path, an {integral} adds the small contributions to work. On a force-position graph, this is signed area.
Potential Energy of a System | {Potential energy} belongs to a system's configuration. Near Earth's surface, gravitational changes can be modeled as ΔU = mgΔy. | A {conservative force} does work that depends only on endpoints, not the path. Its work equals the negative change in associated potential energy. | The {zero level} of potential energy is a choice. Observable energy changes do not depend on where that reference is set.
Sources of Energy | Energy {conservation} means energy is transferred or transformed, not created or destroyed. Some transformations leave less energy available for useful work. | {Renewable} sources are replenished naturally on human timescales. This does not mean every use has no environmental impact. | {Fossil fuels} release stored chemical energy when burned. Compare sources using supply, conversion losses, and environmental effects rather than energy output alone.
