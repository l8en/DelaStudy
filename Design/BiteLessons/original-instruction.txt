Angle relationships | Opposite angles made by two intersecting lines are {vertical} angles. They have equal measures even when the drawing is not to scale. | The angles in a linear pair add to {180} degrees. If one measures 70 degrees, the other measures 110 degrees. | Two {adjacent} angles share a vertex and a side without overlapping interiors. Being adjacent alone does not mean their measures add to 180 degrees.
Parallel lines and transversals | A {transversal} crosses two or more lines at distinct points. It creates angle pairs whose relationships depend on the lines' geometry. | When parallel lines are cut by a transversal, {corresponding angles} are equal. Their matching positions help identify them. | For parallel lines, same-side interior angles are {supplementary}: their measures add to 180 degrees. The parallel-line condition is essential.
Proof and counterexamples | A {proof} establishes a claim using definitions, accepted facts, and logically connected steps. A convincing picture alone is not a proof. | A {counterexample} disproves a universal claim with one valid exception. A rectangle that is not a square disproves the claim that all rectangles are squares. | A {converse} reverses a conditional statement. If a statement is true, its converse is not automatically true.
Triangle angle sums | In Euclidean plane geometry, a triangle's interior angles sum to {180} degrees. Two angles of 50 and 60 degrees leave 70 degrees. | An {exterior angle} of a triangle equals the sum of the two nonadjacent interior angles. It forms a linear pair with the adjacent interior angle. | An {equilateral} triangle has three equal sides and three 60-degree angles. Equal sides imply equal opposite angles.
Congruence criteria | {Congruent} triangles have the same shape and size. Corresponding sides and angles match, even if one triangle is rotated or reflected. | The {SAS} criterion uses two corresponding sides and their included angle. An angle not between the sides does not establish the same condition. | The {AAA} condition establishes similarity, not necessarily congruence. Matching angles alone do not fix a triangle's size.
Isosceles triangles and midsegments | An {isosceles} triangle has at least two equal sides. Its angles opposite those equal sides are equal. | A triangle's {midsegment} joins the midpoints of two sides. It is parallel to the third side and half its length. | A {midpoint} divides a segment into two equal lengths. A point that merely looks central in a sketch needs supporting information.
Scale factors and similarity | {Similar} figures have equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding lengths. Their sizes can differ. | A {scale factor} multiplies every corresponding length. A factor of 3 changes a 2-centimeter side into a 6-centimeter side. | Areas change by the {square} of the scale factor. Enlarging every length by 3 multiplies area by 9, not 3.
The Pythagorean theorem | The {hypotenuse} is opposite a right triangle's right angle. It is the triangle's longest side. | The {Pythagorean theorem} states a² + b² = c² for a right triangle, where c is the hypotenuse. | A 3-4-5 triangle is {right} because 3² + 4² = 5². Check the longest side before applying the converse.
Right-triangle trigonometry | {Sine} of an acute angle equals opposite side divided by hypotenuse. Which side is opposite depends on the chosen angle. | {Cosine} of an acute angle equals adjacent side divided by hypotenuse. The adjacent leg is not the hypotenuse. | {Tangent} of an acute angle equals opposite leg divided by adjacent leg. Use a calculator's correct degree or radian setting.
Arcs and circle angles | A {central angle} has its vertex at the circle's center. Its degree measure matches the measure of its intercepted arc. | An {inscribed angle} has its vertex on the circle. Its measure is half the measure of its intercepted arc. | An angle inscribed in a {semicircle} is a right angle. It intercepts a 180-degree arc and therefore measures 90 degrees.
Circle length and area | A circle's {circumference} is 2πr. It measures the boundary length, not the region inside the circle. | A circle's {area} is πr². Doubling its radius multiplies its area by four. | An {arc length} is a fraction of the circumference. A 90-degree arc is one quarter of a full circle's boundary.
Distance, midpoint, and circles | The {distance formula} comes from the Pythagorean theorem: square the coordinate differences, add them, and take the square root. | A segment's {midpoint} is found by averaging its endpoints' x-coordinates and averaging their y-coordinates. | In (x - h)² + (y - k)² = r², the {center} is (h, k). The right side is radius squared, not radius.
Area by decomposition | {Decomposition} splits a shape into simpler parts with known areas. Add the nonoverlapping parts to recover the whole area. | A {triangle} has area one half times base times perpendicular height. A slanted side is not automatically the height. | For a region with a hole, {subtract} the missing area from the outer area. Keep all measurements in compatible units.
Volumes and surface areas | {Volume} measures space inside a solid and uses cubic units. A rectangular prism has volume length times width times height. | {Surface area} measures the total area of a solid's boundary and uses square units. It is different from volume. | A {net} unfolds a solid's faces into a plane. It can help identify every face that belongs in the surface-area calculation.
Rigid motions and symmetry | A {rigid motion} preserves lengths and angles. Translations, rotations, and reflections keep a figure congruent to its image. | A {reflection} flips points across a line. Each point and its image are equally far from that line on opposite sides. | {Rotational symmetry} means a turn smaller than a full circle can carry a figure onto itself. State the center and angle.
Java types and assignment | An {int} stores an integer in Java, while a double stores a floating-point value. Choose a type that fits the calculation. | {Assignment} stores a value in a variable. After int a = 4; int b = a; a = 9;, b still contains 4. | A {boolean} stores true or false. It represents a logical condition rather than an arbitrary word or number.
Arithmetic and integer division | Java {integer division} discards the fractional part when both operands are integers. The expression 7 / 2 evaluates to 3. | A {remainder} is produced by the % operator. For positive integers, 7 % 2 evaluates to 1. | An {overflow} can occur when an integer result exceeds the type's representable range. Java int arithmetic does not automatically become unlimited-precision arithmetic.
Strings and concatenation | A Java {String} represents a sequence of characters. Its contents are immutable, so operations return new strings rather than changing the original text. | {Concatenation} joins strings using +. When a string is involved, left-to-right evaluation can change how a mixed expression is interpreted. | Use {equals} to compare String contents. The == operator compares references rather than reliably testing whether two strings contain the same text.
Boolean expressions | A {comparison} such as x < 10 produces a boolean value. Assignment with = is not the same as equality comparison with ==. | The {and} operator && requires both conditions to be true. Java skips the right operand when the left operand is false. | The {not} operator ! reverses a boolean value. If ready is true, !ready is false.
Selection and boundary cases | An {if statement} chooses whether to execute code based on a boolean condition. Its body runs only when the condition is true. | An {else} branch runs when the paired if condition is false. Indentation should make the actual pairing clear. | A {boundary case} lies at a cutoff, such as age exactly 16. Test below, at, and above the cutoff to catch < versus ≤ mistakes.
Tracing nested conditions | A {nested condition} places one selection inside another. The inner condition is reached only if execution enters its enclosing branch. | A {trace} follows code in execution order while recording changing values. Do not evaluate branches the program never reaches. | An {else-if chain} selects the first matching branch. Later branches in the same chain are skipped after one is chosen.
Count-controlled loops | A {for loop} combines initialization, a continuation test, and an update. The condition is checked before each iteration. | An {off-by-one error} repeats a loop once too many or too few times. Check its starting value and whether its limit is included. | A loop {counter} tracks progress through repeated steps. For i = 0; i < 3; i++, the body uses 0, 1, and 2.
Accumulators and invariants | An {accumulator} combines values across loop iterations, such as adding each array value to a running total. Initialize it before the loop. | A {loop invariant} is a fact that remains true at a chosen point in each iteration. It helps explain why an algorithm works. | An {initial value} must match the operation. Start a sum at zero and a product at one unless the problem requires something different.
While loops and termination | A {while loop} repeats while its condition is true. Its body may run zero times if the condition is false initially. | A {termination condition} ensures the loop eventually stops. Each iteration should move toward it when the algorithm is intended to finish. | An {infinite loop} never reaches a stopping condition. A forgotten update or a condition that cannot become false can cause one.
Methods and parameters | A {method} groups instructions into a named operation. A clear responsibility makes it easier to test and reuse. | A {parameter} receives an argument value when a method is called. Java passes values, including values that are object references. | A {return value} sends a result back to the caller. Printing a value is not the same as returning it.
Classes and encapsulation | A {class} defines a kind of object through fields, constructors, and methods. An object is an instance of a class. | {Encapsulation} controls access to internal state. Private fields and suitable public methods help preserve an object's rules. | A {constructor} initializes a new object. It has the class's name and no return type, not even void.
References and aliases | A {reference} identifies an object rather than containing the whole object directly. Assigning a reference copies that reference value. | An {alias} is another reference to the same object. A change through one reference can be visible through the other. | The value {null} means a reference points to no object. Accessing an instance member through null can cause a NullPointerException.
Array indices and traversal | An array's first {index} is zero. For an array of length n, the last valid index is n - 1. | The {length} property gives an array's number of elements. It is not the same as its last index. | A {traversal} visits the elements systematically. A loop using i < array.length avoids indexing one position beyond the end.
Searching and edge cases | A {linear search} checks elements in sequence until finding a match or reaching the end. It can work on unsorted data. | A {binary search} repeatedly halves a sorted search range. Its ordering requirement is essential to its correctness. | An {edge case} tests a limit, such as an empty array, one element, or a missing target. Ordinary examples alone may miss failures.
Nested loops and complexity | {Nested loops} place one loop inside another. Count how many times the inner work runs across all outer iterations. | If both loops independently run n times, the repeated body runs {n squared} times. That is quadratic growth in the input size. | {Big O} describes an asymptotic growth bound. It does not specify an exact runtime or guarantee one implementation is faster for every small input.
Build a cash-flow budget | A {cash-flow budget} tracks when money arrives and when payments are due. A monthly total can hide a shortage earlier in the month. | {Income} is money coming in. Use the amount actually available rather than assuming every promised payment has already arrived. | A {surplus} is money left after planned expenses for the period. A shortfall means spending or timing needs to change.
Gross pay and take-home pay | {Gross pay} is earnings before deductions. It is not the amount necessarily deposited into a bank account. | {Take-home pay} is pay remaining after deductions. A budget for spending should reflect this available amount. | A {pay stub} lists earnings and deductions. Check both the current pay period and any year-to-date totals before interpreting it.
Trade-offs and opportunity cost | A {trade-off} gives up one option to gain another. Limited money and time make trade-offs unavoidable. | {Opportunity cost} is the value of the best alternative not chosen. It is not the sum of every possible option. | A {priority} helps decide which trade-offs fit a goal. Compare the effect on essential spending and future commitments before choosing.
Turn a goal into a plan | A {savings goal} identifies an amount and a date. A specific target can be divided into manageable contributions. | A {weekly contribution} can be estimated by dividing the remaining goal by the weeks available. Saving $120 over 12 weeks requires $10 per week before interest. | A {progress check} compares actual savings with the plan. Adjust the amount, timeline, or goal if the original schedule no longer fits.
Plan for irregular costs | An {irregular expense} does not happen every month but can still be expected. Annual fees and seasonal school costs are examples. | A {sinking fund} sets aside money gradually for a known future cost. A $120 annual expense can be planned as $10 each month. | An {emergency fund} is for unplanned needs. It serves a different purpose from savings already committed to a predictable bill.
Simple and compound interest | {Simple interest} is calculated on the original principal. At 5% yearly simple interest, $100 earns $5 each year. | {Compound interest} also applies to previously added interest. Growth depends on the rate, compounding schedule, and time. | The {principal} is the original amount saved or borrowed. Distinguish it from the interest when comparing balances.
Reports and scores are different | A {credit report} records aspects of a person's credit history. It is a collection of information rather than one summary number. | A {credit score} is calculated from credit information using a model. Different models can produce different scores. | A {report error} can affect decisions based on the record. Use an official dispute process and keep copies of relevant evidence.
Credit utilization as a ratio | {Credit utilization} compares a reported revolving balance with the relevant credit limit. A $200 balance on a $1000 limit is 20%. | A {credit limit} is the maximum borrowing allowed on an account. It is not extra income or a target to spend. | A reported {balance} can differ from what is owed today because reporting and payment dates differ. Ratios need the relevant dates and amounts.
Check errors and protect information | {Identity theft} involves misuse of personal information. Unexpected accounts or transactions deserve prompt investigation. | A {credit freeze} restricts access to a credit file for many new-credit checks. It differs from simply changing an account password. | A {dispute} challenges inaccurate report information. Record the issue clearly and keep documentation rather than assuming it will disappear on its own.
Debit, credit, and account fees | A {debit card} generally spends money from a linked account. A credit card instead borrows under an agreement. | A {fee} is a charge for a service or account condition. Read when it applies rather than comparing interest rates alone. | An {overdraft} occurs when a transaction exceeds available account funds under the account's rules. Approval does not necessarily mean the transaction is free.
Rates, balances, and interest | An {interest rate} describes a charge or return over a stated period. Check whether a quoted rate is yearly and how interest is calculated. | A {balance} is the amount in an account or owed at a particular time. Payments, purchases, and interest can change it. | A {minimum payment} is not necessarily enough to avoid interest or repay debt quickly. Compare repayment time and total cost, not just the monthly minimum.
Compare total repayment | {Total repayment} includes the amounts paid across the whole loan. A lower monthly payment can still mean paying more overall. | The {loan term} is the repayment period. Extending it can reduce each payment while increasing interest costs. | Compare {fees} as well as rates. Use the same loan amount and realistic repayment assumptions when evaluating offers.
Read a financial-aid offer | A {grant} generally does not require repayment if its conditions are met. It is different from borrowed aid. | A {loan} must be repaid under its terms. An aid offer that includes loans is not the same as an equally large scholarship. | {Net cost} subtracts grants and scholarships from the relevant attendance costs. Include living costs and required expenses rather than tuition alone.
Plan repayment before borrowing | A {repayment plan} should connect likely payments with a realistic budget. Approval to borrow does not establish affordability. | {Interest accrual} means interest builds up over time under the loan's rules. Check whether it accrues while studying or during payment pauses. | A {scenario} tests what happens if income is lower or costs are higher than expected. Do not rely only on the most optimistic projection.
Contracts, co-signing, and questions | A {contract} sets rights and obligations. Read payment amounts, deadlines, fees, and cancellation terms before agreeing. | A {co-signer} can become responsible for repayment if the borrower does not pay. Co-signing is a financial commitment, not merely a recommendation. | A {clarifying question} should resolve an uncertain term before signing. Keep a copy of the agreement and any written explanation that matters.
