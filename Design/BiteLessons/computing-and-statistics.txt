Computer Science | An {algorithm} is a precise sequence of steps for solving a problem. It must be clear enough to follow consistently. | A {data structure} organizes information so operations such as lookup or insertion can be performed. Different structures suit different tasks. | {Computational thinking} breaks a problem into manageable parts and looks for patterns, useful representations, and repeatable procedures.
Computer Science across the Disciplines | {Computing} supports many fields by storing, processing, and modeling information. A useful solution still needs knowledge of the problem's subject area. | A {simulation} represents a system so researchers can explore possible behavior. Its conclusions depend on assumptions and input data. | {Data analysis} looks for patterns in collected information. Missing, biased, or inaccurate data can produce misleading conclusions even with correct code.
Computer Science and the Future of Society | An {algorithmic decision} can affect opportunities, privacy, and safety. Its consequences depend on the data, design, and setting where it is used. | {Bias} can enter a computing system through data or design choices. Automating a decision does not automatically make it fair. | {Privacy} concerns how information about people is collected, used, and shared. More collected data also creates more responsibility to protect it.
Computational Thinking | {Decomposition} splits a complex problem into smaller parts. Each part should have a clear purpose and relationship to the others. | {Abstraction} keeps the details needed for a task while leaving out irrelevant ones. A useful model is simpler than the full situation. | An {algorithm} turns a solution strategy into precise steps. Test it on ordinary cases and on cases near the limits.
Architecting Solutions with Adaptive Design Reuse in Mind | {Modularity} divides software into parts with clear responsibilities. A change in one part should not require unnecessary changes everywhere else. | An {interface} specifies how parts interact without exposing every internal detail. This makes replacement and reuse easier. | {Reuse} adapts existing components to a suitable task. Check their assumptions rather than treating familiar code as automatically correct.
Evolving Architectures into Useable Products | A {requirement} states something a product must do or a constraint it must satisfy. Clear requirements connect implementation to a real user need. | A {prototype} tests an idea before full implementation. It should answer a question, not merely resemble a finished screen. | {Iteration} uses test results and feedback to improve a design. A working first version is a starting point for evaluation.
Introduction to Data Structures and Algorithms | A {data structure} organizes stored information. Arrays, linked lists, and trees make different operations easier or harder. | An {algorithm} describes steps for an operation such as sorting or searching. The same task can have multiple valid algorithms. | {Complexity} describes how resource use grows with input size. Count the relevant work rather than relying on one machine's timing alone.
Algorithmic Paradigms | {Divide and conquer} splits a problem into smaller instances, solves them, and combines their results. Merge sort is a classic example. | A {greedy algorithm} chooses what appears best at each step. It needs justification because local choices do not always produce a global optimum. | {Dynamic programming} stores solutions to overlapping subproblems so they do not need to be solved repeatedly. The subproblem definitions are crucial.
Computer Science Theory | {Computability} asks which problems can be solved by an algorithm at all. Some precisely stated problems have no general algorithmic solution. | {Complexity theory} studies how much time or space a problem requires as input size increases. Solvable does not always mean practical. | An {automaton} is a mathematical model of computation. Different models capture different kinds of state and processing power.
Models of Computation | A {model of computation} defines the rules and capabilities of an abstract machine. It helps compare what kinds of problems machines can solve. | A {finite-state machine} has a limited set of states and changes state in response to inputs. It cannot store an unlimited history. | A {Turing machine} uses an idealized tape and control rules. It is a theoretical model, not a specification for a modern laptop.
Parallel Programming Models | {Parallel computing} performs parts of a computation at the same time. Not every task can be divided into independent pieces. | In {shared memory}, multiple workers access a common memory space. Coordination is needed to prevent conflicting updates. | In {message passing}, workers exchange information explicitly. Communication and synchronization can limit the benefit of adding workers.
Applications of Programming Models | A {programming model} shapes how a developer expresses a computation. Choose one that fits the task and execution environment. | {Data parallelism} applies similar operations to different portions of data. Processing separate image pixels is a typical example. | {Task parallelism} runs different tasks concurrently. Dependencies must be respected so one task does not consume another's unfinished result.
Computer Systems Organization | The {CPU} executes instructions and coordinates computation. Memory and input-output devices support its work. | {Main memory} holds programs and data in active use. Persistent storage keeps information after power is removed. | A {bus} or interconnect carries data and control signals between components. System performance depends on communication as well as computation.
Machine-Level Program Representation | {Machine code} consists of instructions a processor can execute. Different processor architectures use different instruction sets. | {Assembly language} gives symbolic names to machine-level operations. It is close to the processor rather than a high-level description of the task. | A {register} is a small, fast storage location in the processor. Instructions commonly operate on registers and values loaded from memory.
Processor Architectures | An {instruction set architecture} defines the instructions and visible behavior a processor supports. It is distinct from the internal implementation. | A {pipeline} overlaps stages of instruction execution. Dependencies and branches can prevent it from running at full throughput. | A {cache} stores recently or frequently used data near the processor. Access patterns affect how useful it is.
Definitions of Statistics, Probability, and Key Terms | A {population} is the whole group a study concerns. A sample is the subset from which data are actually collected. | A {parameter} describes a population, while a statistic describes a sample. A sample average is a statistic, not automatically the population average. | {Probability} quantifies uncertainty under a defined model. It ranges from zero for impossible events to one for certain events in that model.
Experimental Design and Ethics | {Random assignment} distributes participants among treatment groups by chance. It helps separate treatment effects from preexisting group differences. | A {confounding variable} affects the outcome and is mixed up with the factor being studied. It can create an alternative explanation. | {Informed consent} protects participants' ability to choose. A statistically useful design must still meet ethical requirements.
Sampling Experiment | A {sample} is a subset of the population being studied. Define that population before deciding how to select observations. | A {random sample} uses chance in selection. It helps reduce selection bias but does not remove every possible source of error. | {Sampling variability} means different samples can give different statistics. One sample result is not guaranteed to equal the population value.
Stem-and-Leaf Graphs (Stemplots), Line Graphs, and Bar Graphs | A {stem-and-leaf plot} splits each numerical observation into a leading stem and a final leaf. It can show shape while retaining individual values. | A {bar graph} compares categories using bar heights. The categories need not be numerical intervals. | A {line graph} connects ordered observations, often across time. Check whether the connecting lines suggest a meaningful sequence.
Measures of the Center of the Data | The {mean} is the sum of values divided by their count. An extreme value can pull it toward that extreme. | The {median} is the middle value after sorting, or the average of the two middle values for an even count. | The {mode} is a most frequent value. A dataset can have more than one mode, or no useful single mode.
Descriptive Statistics | {Descriptive statistics} summarize observed data. They do not by themselves establish causes or justify claims about an unobserved population. | The {center} describes a typical location, such as a mean or median. Choose a measure suited to the distribution. | The {spread} describes variability. Datasets with the same center can still have very different ranges and standard deviations.
Terminology | An {outcome} is one possible result of a random process. The sample space contains all possible outcomes in the model. | An {event} is a set of outcomes. Its probability combines the probabilities of the outcomes it includes. | The {complement} of an event contains the outcomes outside it. An event's probability plus its complement's probability equals one.
Contingency Tables | A {contingency table} records counts for combinations of categorical variables. Each cell represents one combination of categories. | A {marginal total} adds across a row or column. It summarizes one variable without restricting the other. | A {conditional probability} restricts the denominator to the group given in the condition. Changing that group can change the probability.
Probability Topics | Two events are {independent} when knowing one occurred does not change the other's probability. Independence must be justified, not assumed. | Events are {mutually exclusive} when they cannot occur together. Two positive-probability events cannot be both mutually exclusive and independent. | A {conditional probability} is the probability of an event given another event. The condition changes the relevant sample space.
Probability Distribution Function (PDF) for a Discrete Random Variable | A {discrete random variable} takes countable possible values. A die's result is a simple example. | A {probability distribution} assigns probabilities to the possible values. Each probability is nonnegative, and all probabilities add to one. | The {expected value} is the probability-weighted mean. It need not be a possible single observed result.
Hypergeometric Distribution | A {hypergeometric distribution} models the count of successes in a fixed-size sample drawn without replacement from a finite population. | Sampling {without replacement} changes the composition remaining after each draw. The draws are generally not independent. | Specify the {population size}, total successes, and sample size before calculating a probability. These are different quantities, not interchangeable inputs.
Discrete Distribution (Dice Experiment Using Three Regular Dice) | A {random variable} can represent the sum of three dice. Define it before listing possible outcomes or probabilities. | {Relative frequency} is the observed fraction of trials with a result. It estimates a probability but varies from sample to sample. | The {theoretical distribution} follows from the model of fair, independent dice. Sums are not equally likely even though individual die faces are.
The Standard Normal Distribution | A {normal distribution} is symmetric and bell-shaped. Its mean and standard deviation determine its location and spread. | The {standard normal} distribution has mean zero and standard deviation one. It provides a shared scale for normal-model calculations. | A {z-score} equals the value minus the mean, divided by the standard deviation. It states how many standard deviations a value lies from the mean.
Normal Distribution (Lap Times) | A {normal model} is an approximation to data, not a guarantee that real lap times form a perfect bell curve. | A {z-score} converts a lap time to standard-deviation units relative to the chosen mean and standard deviation. | A {tail probability} represents times beyond a cutoff. For performance comparisons, remember that a smaller lap time usually means a faster lap.
Normal Distribution (Pinkie Length) | A {measurement} needs a consistent procedure and unit. Different ways of measuring a finger can introduce differences unrelated to the population. | A {histogram} helps assess whether a normal model is reasonable. Check its shape rather than assuming all measurements are normally distributed. | A {standard deviation} describes spread around the mean. A normal-model probability also requires the specific cutoff and its relation to the mean.
Histograms, Frequency Polygons, and Time Series Graphs | A {histogram} groups numerical values into intervals. Bar areas or heights must be interpreted according to the graph's scale. | A {frequency polygon} connects frequencies plotted at interval midpoints. It can make distribution shapes easier to compare. | A {time series} plots observations in time order. Rearranging the values would destroy information about trends and timing.
Mean or Expected Value and Standard Deviation | The {expected value} of a discrete random variable is the sum of each value times its probability. | The {variance} is the expected squared distance from the mean. Squaring keeps positive and negative deviations from canceling. | The {standard deviation} is the square root of variance. It uses the same units as the random variable.
Continuous Probability Functions | A {continuous random variable} can take values over an interval. Probabilities are assigned to intervals rather than individual points. | A {density curve} has total area one and never falls below zero. Probability is area under it, not the curve's height alone. | The probability of one exact value is {zero} in a continuous model. This does not make an observed measurement impossible.
The Central Limit Theorem for Sample Means (Averages) | A {sampling distribution} describes a statistic across repeated samples of the same size. It is not the distribution of individual observations. | Under suitable conditions, the {central limit theorem} makes sample means approximately normal for large samples, even when the original population is not normal. | The mean's {standard error} is σ/√n for independent observations with population standard deviation σ. Larger samples reduce this typical sampling variation.
A Single Population Mean using the Normal Distribution | A {confidence interval} gives a range using a method with a stated long-run coverage rate. It is not a guarantee for every sample. | A {z interval} for a mean requires known population standard deviation and suitable conditions. When it is estimated, a t method is often appropriate. | The {margin of error} combines a critical value with the standard error. Larger samples generally reduce it when the other conditions stay the same.
Confidence Interval (Women's Heights) | A {sample mean} summarizes the measured heights in the sample. It estimates, but need not equal, the mean for the defined population. | The {target population} must be stated. A convenience sample from one classroom cannot automatically represent all women. | A {confidence interval} describes uncertainty from the sampling method under its assumptions. It does not correct a biased measurement or selection process.
Null and Alternative Hypotheses | The {null hypothesis} states a reference claim about a population parameter. A test measures how compatible the data are with that claim. | The {alternative hypothesis} states the competing direction or difference of interest. Choose it before using the results to decide what counts as surprising. | A {p-value} is the probability, assuming the null model, of results at least as extreme as observed. It is not the probability the null hypothesis is true.
Scatter Plots | A {scatter plot} displays paired numerical observations. Each point represents one case's values on two variables. | {Correlation} measures the direction and strength of a linear association. A strong association does not establish cause and effect. | An {outlier} lies unusually far from the general pattern. Investigate it rather than automatically deleting it.
Regression (Fuel Efficiency) | A {regression line} predicts an outcome such as fuel efficiency from an explanatory variable. Its suitability depends on the data and assumptions. | A {residual} is observed fuel efficiency minus predicted fuel efficiency. A pattern in residuals can reveal a poor model fit. | {Extrapolation} predicts beyond the data's observed input range. A relationship fitted to one set of vehicles may not extend to very different vehicles.
