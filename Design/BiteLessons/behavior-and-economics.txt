What Is Psychology? | {Psychology} scientifically studies behavior and mental processes. It uses evidence rather than relying only on intuition about why people act. | {Empirical evidence} comes from observation and measurement. A convincing personal story is not a substitute for systematic evidence. | {Behavior} is what organisms do. Psychologists connect observable behavior with mental processes while recognizing the limits of each measurement.
Why Is Research Important? | A {hypothesis} makes a testable prediction. Research can challenge a plausible idea rather than merely collect examples supporting it. | {Correlation} describes an association between variables. It does not by itself establish that one variable causes the other. | A controlled {experiment} manipulates a variable and compares outcomes. Good design helps rule out alternative explanations for a result.
Ethics | {Informed consent} means participants receive meaningful information about a study before agreeing to join, subject to the relevant ethical rules. | {Confidentiality} protects participants' identifying information. Research reports should not expose private details unnecessarily. | An {ethics review} evaluates risks, safeguards, and participant protections. Scientific interest alone does not justify harmful or coercive methods.
Human Genetics | A {gene} is a DNA sequence involved in producing a functional product. Genes influence traits through interactions with other genes and environments. | A person's {genotype} is their genetic makeup. Their phenotype is an observable characteristic shaped by genetic and environmental influences. | {Heritability} describes variation within a studied population under particular conditions. It is not the percentage of an individual's trait caused by genes.
Parts of the Nervous System | The {central nervous system} includes the brain and spinal cord. It processes information and helps coordinate responses. | The {peripheral nervous system} connects the central nervous system with the rest of the body through nerves outside the brain and spinal cord. | The {autonomic nervous system} helps regulate internal functions such as heart rate. Its sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions have different, coordinated roles.
The Endocrine System | The {endocrine system} uses glands to release hormones into the bloodstream. Hormones act on cells with suitable receptors. | A {hormone} is a chemical signal that influences target cells. Its effects depend on the receiving cells and context. | {Feedback} helps regulate hormone levels. A response can reduce the signal that initially triggered hormone release.
Sensation versus Perception | {Sensation} begins when sensory receptors detect information such as light or sound. Detection alone is not the same as interpretation. | {Perception} organizes and interprets sensory information. Expectations and context can affect what a person experiences. | {Sensory adaptation} is reduced sensitivity to a constant stimulus. A persistent smell may become less noticeable even though it remains present.
Hearing | A sound's {frequency} relates to perceived pitch. Higher-frequency waves generally sound higher in pitch. | Sound wave {amplitude} relates to loudness, though perception also depends on frequency and other factors. | The {cochlea} is an inner-ear structure where hair cells convert mechanical vibrations into signals used by the nervous system.
Gestalt Principles of Perception | The principle of {proximity} describes a tendency to perceive nearby elements as a group. Spacing can change how the same marks are organized. | The principle of {similarity} groups elements that look alike. Shared shape or color can make separate items feel connected. | {Closure} is the tendency to perceive an incomplete shape as a whole. Perception actively organizes information instead of copying every detail literally.
What Is Learning? | {Learning} is a relatively lasting change in behavior or knowledge resulting from experience. Not every temporary change counts as learning. | {Classical conditioning} links stimuli so that one can come to evoke a response associated with another. | {Operant conditioning} changes behavior through its consequences. Reinforcement increases a behavior; punishment decreases it under the technical definitions.
How Memory Functions | {Encoding} puts information into a form that can be stored. Attention and how material is processed affect this first stage. | {Storage} maintains information over time. Memory is not a perfect recording and can change with later experiences. | {Retrieval} brings stored information back into use. A retrieval failure does not always mean the information was never stored.
Ways to Enhance Memory | {Retrieval practice} means trying to recall information rather than only rereading it. Checking your answer afterward helps correct mistakes. | {Spacing} distributes study across time instead of putting all practice into one sitting. The best schedule depends on the learning task. | {Elaboration} connects new information to meaning, examples, or existing knowledge. A connection should be accurate, not just memorable.
What Is Lifespan Development? | {Lifespan development} studies changes and continuities from conception through old age. Development does not end after childhood. | {Physical development} includes changes in the body and brain. Cognitive and social development interact with these changes. | A {longitudinal study} follows the same participants over time. It differs from comparing different age groups at one moment.
Stages of Development | {Developmental milestones} describe common patterns in growth. Their timing varies, so a single age is not a universal deadline. | {Cognitive development} concerns changes in thinking, learning, and problem solving. Theories offer models rather than complete descriptions of every individual. | {Attachment} concerns emotional bonds with caregivers. Relationships and environments interact with a child's developing abilities.
Death and Dying | {Grief} is a response to loss that can involve emotional, physical, and social changes. People do not all experience it in one fixed sequence. | {Hospice} focuses on comfort and support near the end of life. It includes attention to the person and often their family. | An {advance directive} records preferences for future medical decisions. Specific legal requirements depend on location and circumstances.
What Is Sociology? | {Sociology} studies social groups, institutions, and interactions. It connects individual experiences with patterns in social life. | A {society} is a population sharing a social life and institutions within a territory or organized community. It is not simply any crowd. | A {social institution} is an organized pattern meeting recurring social needs. Education and family are examples sociologists investigate.
Theoretical Perspectives in Sociology | {Functionalism} examines how parts of society contribute to its operation and stability. It also considers dysfunctions and unintended effects. | {Conflict theory} focuses on unequal power and resources. It asks whose interests social arrangements serve and how inequality is reproduced. | {Symbolic interactionism} studies meaning in everyday interaction. Shared symbols and interpretations shape how people respond to one another.
Why Study Sociology? | The {sociological imagination} connects personal experiences with wider social conditions. A private difficulty can also reflect a public pattern. | A {social pattern} is a recurring relationship or behavior across a group. It is not proof that every individual behaves the same way. | {Evidence} helps test explanations of social life. Familiar stereotypes are not adequate substitutes for systematic investigation.
Approaches to Sociological Research | A {research question} defines what a study seeks to understand. Clear questions guide the choice of evidence and method. | An {operational definition} explains how a concept will be measured. Different measures of the same idea can produce different results. | A {hypothesis} predicts a relationship that can be tested. Researchers must be willing to revise it when evidence does not support it.
Research Methods | A {survey} collects responses using questions. Wording, sampling, and nonresponse affect what its results can tell us. | An {ethnography} studies social life through detailed observation and participation. It can reveal context that a short questionnaire misses. | {Secondary data} were collected for an earlier purpose. Researchers must evaluate whether those data fit the new question.
Ethical Concerns | {Informed consent} requires participants to understand relevant study information and choose freely whether to take part. | {Confidentiality} protects identifying information. Small groups can be identifiable even when researchers leave out names. | Research {ethics} requires weighing risks, avoiding deception where possible, and protecting participants. Collecting useful data does not override these responsibilities.
What Is Culture? | {Culture} includes shared meanings, practices, values, and material objects. It is learned and changes over time. | A {norm} is an expectation for behavior. Norms can differ across groups and settings, even within the same society. | {Ethnocentrism} judges another culture mainly by one's own standards. Understanding context helps avoid treating familiar practices as automatically superior.
High, Low, Pop, Sub, Counter-culture and Cultural Change | {Popular culture} includes widely shared cultural activities and products. Labels about cultural status also reflect social power. | A {subculture} has distinctive practices within a wider society. It does not necessarily reject the wider culture's central values. | A {counterculture} challenges important mainstream values. Cultural groups are diverse rather than perfectly uniform blocks.
Theoretical Perspectives on Culture | {Functionalism} asks how shared cultural practices support coordination and social stability. It can also examine their unintended consequences. | {Conflict theory} asks how culture reflects power and inequality. Whose tastes count as prestigious is a sociological question. | {Symbolic interactionism} examines how people create and negotiate meanings through everyday use of symbols and language.
Types of Societies | A {hunting and gathering} society relies primarily on wild plants and animals. Such societies have varied arrangements, not one universal lifestyle. | An {industrial society} organizes much production around machinery and large-scale manufacturing. Its growth changes work, cities, and social relationships. | A {postindustrial society} gives information and services a larger economic role. Material production and inequality do not simply disappear.
Theoretical Perspectives on Society | {Durkheim} examined how social bonds hold societies together, including bonds based on similarity and those based on interdependence. | {Marx} emphasized class conflict and relationships around production. He connected economic arrangements with wider social power. | {Weber} examined authority, meaning, and rationalization. His approach considered more than economic resources alone.
Social Constructions of Reality | A {social construction} is a meaning or category produced through social interaction. Socially created meanings can still have real consequences. | A {status} is a social position, such as student or sibling. A person occupies several statuses at once. | A {role} is a set of expectations connected with a status. Conflicting expectations can create role strain or role conflict.
Theories of Self-Development | The {self} develops through interaction as people learn to see themselves in relation to others. It is not shaped in isolation. | The {looking-glass self} describes how our imagined judgments of others contribute to our self-understanding. Those imagined judgments may be inaccurate. | {Role-taking} involves considering another person's perspective. It helps people anticipate responses and participate in shared social situations.
Agents of Socialization | {Socialization} is the process of learning a society's expectations, meanings, and practices. It continues beyond childhood. | The {family} is often an early agent of socialization. Its influence interacts with schools, peers, media, and other institutions. | A {peer group} consists of people with similar age or social position. It can reinforce or challenge lessons learned elsewhere.
Socialization Across the Life Course | {Life transitions} such as entering school or starting work bring new roles and expectations. Socialization continues as these roles change. | {Resocialization} involves learning substantially different expectations and behaviors. It may occur when entering a new institution or social environment. | A {total institution} organizes many aspects of residents' lives in one setting under a central authority. Such control can reshape identity and daily behavior.
What Is Economics, and Why Is It Important? | {Scarcity} means wants exceed available resources. It exists even when people or societies are wealthy. | {Opportunity cost} is the value of the best alternative given up when making a choice. It is not every possible alternative added together. | The {division of labor} splits production into specialized tasks. Specialization can raise output but also creates dependence on exchange and coordination.
How Economists Use Theories and Models to Understand Economic Issues | An economic {model} simplifies reality to study a relationship. Its usefulness depends on its assumptions and the question being asked. | {Ceteris paribus} means holding other relevant factors constant. It helps isolate one relationship, not claim that other factors never change. | A {positive statement} describes or predicts what happens. A normative statement expresses a judgment about what ought to happen.
How To Organize Economies: An Overview of Economic Systems | A {market economy} relies heavily on decentralized decisions and exchange. Prices help coordinate buyers and sellers. | A {command economy} relies heavily on centralized decisions about production and allocation. Actual economies usually combine several organizing methods. | A {mixed economy} combines market activity with government roles. The balance differs across places, industries, and public goals.
How Individuals Make Choices Based on Their Budget Constraint | A {budget constraint} shows the combinations a person can afford at given prices and income. Choosing more of one good may require less of another. | A choice's {opportunity cost} is the best alternative forgone. A price change can alter that trade-off even if income stays fixed. | {Marginal analysis} compares the added benefit and added cost of one more unit. Total benefits alone do not identify the next best choice.
The Production Possibilities Frontier and Social Choices | A {production possibilities frontier} shows maximum combinations of two outputs given resources and technology. Points beyond it are not currently attainable. | A point {inside} the frontier indicates unused resources or inefficient production under the model. Moving toward the frontier can increase output without new resources. | An {outward shift} means productive capacity has grown. New technology or additional resources can make previously unattainable combinations possible.
Confronting Objections to the Economic Approach | Economic models use {simplification} to study choices. A simplified account need not claim that people calculate every decision perfectly. | {Incentives} change the costs or benefits of actions. People's responses can vary, and unintended effects are possible. | {Efficiency} and fairness are different questions. A policy can increase total output while distributing its benefits unequally.
Demand, Supply, and Equilibrium in Markets for Goods and Services | {Demand} relates a good's price to how much buyers are willing and able to buy, holding other factors constant. | {Supply} relates price to how much sellers are willing and able to offer, holding other factors constant. | {Equilibrium} occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. It is a market outcome, not a judgment that the result is fair.
Changes in Equilibrium Price and Quantity: The Four-Step Process | A change in a good's own price causes movement {along a curve}. It does not by itself shift that good's demand or supply curve. | A demand or supply {shift} comes from another factor, such as income, input costs, or technology. Identify which curve is affected first. | The {new equilibrium} comes from the curves' new intersection. If both curves shift, one outcome may be uncertain without information about relative shift sizes.
Demand, Supply, and Efficiency | {Consumer surplus} is the difference between what buyers would be willing to pay and what they actually pay, added across units purchased. | {Producer surplus} is the difference between the received price and the minimum sellers would accept, added across units sold. | {Deadweight loss} is lost total surplus from trades that do not occur or resources misallocated relative to the model's efficient outcome.
Demand and Supply at Work in Labor Markets | In a labor market, workers {supply} labor and employers demand it. Wages are a price for labor services. | Employers' demand for labor is a {derived demand}: it depends partly on demand for the goods and services workers produce. | A labor market {equilibrium} reflects supply and demand under the model. Institutions, bargaining, discrimination, and legal rules can also affect observed outcomes.
Demand and Supply in Financial Markets | Financial markets connect {savers} with borrowers. Interest rates influence the amount of funds supplied and demanded. | The {interest rate} is a cost of borrowing and a return for providing funds. Risk and loan terms help explain different rates. | {Borrowers} demand funds for uses such as investment or consumption. A change in expected returns can shift that demand.
The Market System as an Efficient Mechanism for Information | A {price} can communicate information about relative scarcity. Buyers and sellers can respond without knowing every detail behind the change. | A {shortage} means quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price. It can create pressure for the price to rise. | A {surplus} means quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the current price. It can create pressure for the price to fall.
Price Elasticity of Demand and Price Elasticity of Supply | {Price elasticity} compares a percentage change in quantity with a percentage change in price. It measures responsiveness, not simply a curve's slope. | Demand is {elastic} when quantity responds proportionally more than price, using the absolute value of elasticity. | Demand is {inelastic} when quantity responds proportionally less than price. Availability of substitutes and adjustment time affect responsiveness.
Elasticity and Pricing | {Total revenue} is price times quantity sold. Raising price does not automatically raise revenue because quantity demanded may fall. | With {elastic demand}, a price increase reduces total revenue, holding the demand curve fixed. The percentage drop in quantity is larger. | With {inelastic demand}, a price increase raises total revenue, holding the demand curve fixed. The percentage drop in quantity is smaller.
Elasticity in Areas Other Than Price | {Income elasticity} measures how quantity demanded responds to a percentage change in income. A negative value characterizes an inferior good. | {Cross-price elasticity} measures how demand for one good responds to another good's price. Substitutes generally have a positive value. | {Complements} are goods often used together. A rise in one good's price can reduce demand for the other, giving negative cross-price elasticity.
Consumption Choices | {Utility} describes satisfaction in a consumer-choice model. It is a modeling concept, not a directly comparable measure of different people's happiness. | {Marginal utility} is the additional satisfaction from one more unit. It often decreases as consumption of the same good rises. | A consumer compares marginal utility per {dollar} across purchases when allocating a fixed budget in the model.
How Changes in Income and Prices Affect Consumption Choices | An {income effect} occurs because a price change changes purchasing power. The same money can buy a different amount. | A {substitution effect} occurs because relative prices change. Consumers tend to substitute toward the relatively cheaper option. | A {normal good} has higher demand when income rises, other things equal. For an inferior good, demand falls as income rises.
Behavioral Economics: An Alternative Framework for Consumer Choice | {Behavioral economics} studies how psychology and decision patterns affect economic choices. People do not always behave like perfectly informed calculators. | {Framing} changes how a choice is presented. Equivalent descriptions can lead people to choose differently. | {Loss aversion} describes a tendency to weigh losses more heavily than comparable gains. It is a behavioral pattern, not a guarantee about every individual.
Explicit and Implicit Costs, and Accounting and Economic Profit | An {explicit cost} is a direct monetary payment, such as wages or rent. It appears in ordinary accounting records. | An {implicit cost} is an opportunity cost of using owned resources, such as the income an owner gives up by working in their business. | {Economic profit} subtracts both explicit and implicit costs from revenue. Accounting profit generally subtracts explicit costs only.
Costs in the Short Run | A {fixed cost} does not vary with output in the short run. It can still change when the business adjusts its scale over a longer period. | A {variable cost} changes with output. Materials used in production are a common example. | {Marginal cost} is the additional cost of producing one more unit. It differs from average cost across all units.
Costs in the Long Run | In the {long run}, a firm can adjust all inputs. This is defined by adjustment possibilities rather than a universal number of months. | {Economies of scale} occur when average cost falls as output increases over the relevant range. | {Diseconomies of scale} occur when average cost rises as scale expands. Coordination problems can outweigh the benefits of greater size.
Measuring the Size of the Economy: Gross Domestic Product | {GDP} measures the value of final goods and services produced within a country over a period. It counts production, not every financial transaction. | {Final goods} are purchased for end use. Excluding separately counted intermediate inputs avoids counting the same production multiple times. | The {expenditure approach} adds consumption, investment, government purchases, and net exports. Transfers are not government purchases of newly produced goods.
Tracking Real GDP over Time | {Nominal GDP} values production at current prices. It can rise because prices rise even when production does not. | {Real GDP} adjusts for price changes to help compare production across time. The chosen price basis matters. | {Real GDP per capita} divides price-adjusted output by population. It is an average, not a measure of how output is distributed.
How Well GDP Measures the Well-Being of Society | {GDP} measures market production, not overall well-being. A higher total does not establish that every person is better off. | {Unpaid work} such as much household care is generally omitted from GDP. Its absence from the measure does not make it valueless. | {Distribution} matters when interpreting averages. The same GDP per person can accompany very different inequality and living conditions.
The Relatively Recent Arrival of Economic Growth | {Economic growth} is a sustained increase in an economy's productive output. Per-person growth distinguishes it from population increase alone. | {Industrialization} increased production through changes in technology, energy use, and organization. Its effects and timing differed across societies. | {Compounding} makes small sustained growth rates matter over long periods. Each year's increase builds on the larger previous amount.
Components of Economic Growth | {Labor productivity} is output per worker or per hour. Increasing it can raise output without requiring proportionally more work time. | {Human capital} includes workers' knowledge and skills. Education and experience can contribute to it. | {Physical capital} includes productive equipment and structures. Technology and institutions influence how effectively capital and labor are used.
Economic Convergence | {Convergence} occurs when poorer economies grow faster than richer ones and reduce the income gap. It is not guaranteed. | {Diminishing returns} can make additional capital especially productive where little capital is initially available, other things equal. | {Institutions} and conditions affect whether catch-up growth occurs. Investment, education, infrastructure, and political stability can shape outcomes.
How Economists Define and Compute Unemployment Rate | The {labor force} includes employed people and unemployed people actively seeking work under the measure's definitions. It does not include every adult. | The {unemployment rate} is unemployed people divided by the labor force, multiplied by 100. Its denominator is not the total population. | {Discouraged workers} have stopped searching and are not counted as unemployed in the standard headline measure. That affects how the rate should be interpreted.
What Causes Changes in Unemployment over the Short Run | {Cyclical unemployment} rises and falls with the business cycle. Weak overall demand can reduce hiring and increase layoffs. | A {recession} involves a broad decline in economic activity. Its labor-market effects can continue after output begins recovering. | {Aggregate demand} is total planned spending in the economy at different price levels. A fall can reduce firms' need for workers in the short run.
What Causes Changes in Unemployment over the Long Run | {Frictional unemployment} occurs while workers search for jobs or move between them. Some search is part of a changing labor market. | {Structural unemployment} reflects mismatches between available jobs and workers' skills, locations, or other characteristics. | The {natural rate} includes frictional and structural unemployment in the model. It is not necessarily fixed or desirable for every worker.
Tracking Inflation | {Inflation} is a sustained rise in the general price level. An increase in the price of one product alone is not the same thing. | A {price index} tracks the cost of a defined set of goods and services relative to a reference period. | {Purchasing power} falls when prices rise while nominal income stays unchanged. The same dollars buy fewer goods and services.
How the U.S. and Other Countries Experience Inflation | {Disinflation} means inflation is slowing. Prices can still be rising, just at a lower rate. | {Deflation} is a decline in the general price level. It is different from slower positive inflation. | {Hyperinflation} is extremely rapid inflation. It can disrupt saving, pricing, contracts, and everyday exchange.
Indexing and Its Limitations | {Indexing} adjusts a payment or value using a price measure. It aims to preserve purchasing power as measured prices change. | A {cost-of-living adjustment} links income or benefits to an index. A household's actual spending pattern may differ from the index's basket. | {Nominal values} are measured in current dollars. Real values adjust for prices, but the result depends on the chosen index.
