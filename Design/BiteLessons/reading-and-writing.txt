"Reading" to Understand and Respond | A {rhetorical situation} includes the audience, purpose, context, and form of a message. These features shape how a text works. | The {audience} is whom a message addresses. A writer's choices should fit what those readers know, need, or expect. | The {purpose} is what a message aims to accomplish. Explaining a process and persuading someone to act require different choices.
Writing Process: Thinking Critically About a “Text” | {Critical reading} asks how a text creates meaning, not only what it says. Notice evidence, assumptions, and choices of language or form. | An {annotation} records a useful observation or question beside a passage. Marking everything does not identify what matters most. | A {response} should connect an interpretation with specific evidence. Explain how the detail supports your point instead of merely quoting it.
Portfolio: Tracing Writing Development | A writing {portfolio} collects selected work to show learning and development. Its value comes from purposeful selection, not simply volume. | A {reflection} explains what changed and why. Point to evidence in drafts rather than declaring improvement without examples. | A {revision goal} identifies a specific next step, such as strengthening evidence or clarifying organization. It is more useful than simply promising to write better.
Identity and Expression | {Identity} includes how people understand themselves and are understood by others. Writing can explore those relationships rather than reduce them to one label. | A writer's {voice} emerges through choices such as vocabulary, rhythm, and perspective. Different situations can call for different voices. | {Context} shapes expression. Language that works within one community may need explanation for a different audience.
Writing Process: Tracing the Beginnings of Literacy | A {literacy narrative} explores an experience with reading, writing, or language. It connects an event with its significance for the writer. | A {scene} uses concrete action and detail to let readers experience a moment. Choose details that support the narrative's focus. | {Reflection} explains why the experience matters. A sequence of events alone does not show what the writer learned or reconsidered.
Portfolio: A Literacy Artifact | A {literacy artifact} is an object or text connected to language learning, such as a marked book or an early piece of writing. | {Analysis} explains what the artifact reveals about a learning experience. Description alone does not make that connection. | A {caption} can identify an artifact and explain why it belongs in a portfolio. Give enough context for an unfamiliar reader.
Exploring the Past to Understand the Present | A {personal narrative} uses a focused experience to explore meaning. It need not tell the writer's entire life story. | {Perspective} affects how an event is remembered and described. A narrator's account is situated rather than a complete record of everyone's experience. | A meaningful {detail} supports the narrative's central insight. Including every remembered fact can weaken its focus.
Writing Process: Making the Personal Public | A {central insight} gives a personal narrative direction. It explains why readers beyond the writer should care about the experience. | {Organization} shapes how readers encounter events and reflection. Chronological order is useful but not the only possible structure. | {Revision} can change the narrative's focus, order, and development. Correcting punctuation alone is editing, not a full revision.
Portfolio: Filtered Memories | {Memory} is selective rather than a perfect recording. A responsible narrative distinguishes remembered experience from independently verified facts. | A {reflection} can acknowledge gaps or changed understanding. Uncertainty need not be hidden to make a story meaningful. | An {artifact} or outside account can add context to a memory. It may complicate rather than simply confirm the writer's first version.
Profiles as Inspiration | A {profile} presents a subject through a focused angle. It combines research and description rather than listing every available fact. | An {angle} is the particular focus that gives a profile direction. It helps determine which scenes, interviews, and details belong. | {Observation} records what the writer can actually see or hear. Keep it distinct from an assumption about the subject's thoughts.
Writing Process: Focusing on the Angle of Your Subject | An {interview} gathers a subject's words and perspective. Open-ended questions often invite fuller answers than questions requiring only yes or no. | {Research} adds context and checks claims. One interview should not automatically be treated as a complete or unquestionable account. | A profile's {structure} should develop its angle. Arrange scenes, background, and quotations to help readers understand the subject.
Portfolio: Subject as a Reflection of Self | A writer's {choices} influence how a profile represents its subject. Selecting and omitting details are part of that responsibility. | {Reflection} can examine how the writer's expectations changed during research. Use specific moments rather than broad claims of personal growth. | {Revision} should check whether the subject is portrayed accurately and fairly. A compelling story does not excuse invented details.
Making a Case: Defining a Position Argument | A {claim} states an arguable position. A factual topic alone does not tell readers what the writer wants them to accept. | {Evidence} supports a claim with relevant information. Explain the reasoning that connects it to the position. | A {counterargument} presents a serious alternative or objection. Addressing it fairly can strengthen an argument and clarify its limits.
Writing Process: Creating a Position Argument | A {thesis} states an essay's central position. It should be specific enough to guide evidence and organization. | A {reason} explains why a reader should accept the claim. Support each important reason rather than relying on repetition. | A {rebuttal} responds to an objection with evidence and reasoning. It should address the actual objection rather than a weaker substitute.
Portfolio: Growth in the Development of Argument | An {argument portfolio} can show changes in claims, evidence, and reasoning across drafts. Include work that makes those changes visible. | A {reflection} explains a revision decision and its effect. Compare particular passages instead of only saying the final draft is stronger. | A {transfer goal} identifies how a writing strategy can be used in a new situation. State when the strategy will be useful and why.
Breaking the Whole into Its Parts | {Rhetorical analysis} examines how a message works for its audience and purpose. It is different from simply agreeing or disagreeing with the message. | An {appeal} is a way of persuading, such as establishing credibility, using reasoning, or engaging emotion. Appeals can work together. | A {detail} becomes analytical evidence when you explain its effect. Naming a technique alone does not show how it shapes meaning.
Writing Process: Thinking Critically about Rhetoric | A {rhetorical choice} is a decision about language, evidence, form, or presentation. Analyze it in relation to audience and purpose. | An analytical {claim} explains how a text works. It should be supported by specific passages or features. | {Context} helps explain why a choice may persuade a particular audience. Avoid assuming all readers respond in the same way.
Portfolio: How Thinking Critically about Rhetoric Affects Intellectual Growth | {Reflection} examines how rhetorical analysis changed the writer's reading or composing decisions. Concrete examples make that learning visible. | A {comparison} between drafts can reveal changed assumptions about an audience. Explain the reason for the change. | {Transfer} means adapting a learned strategy to a new context. Copying a technique unchanged may not fit the next task.
Introducing Research and Research Evidence | A {research question} defines what an investigation seeks to understand. It should be narrow enough to guide a manageable search. | {Evidence} must be relevant and credible for the claim being made. A source's reputation alone does not make every passage useful. | {Synthesis} connects information from multiple sources. It explains their relationships rather than presenting a disconnected list of summaries.
Writing Process: Integrating Research | A {quotation} uses a source's exact words and needs accurate attribution. Use it when the wording itself matters. | A {paraphrase} restates a source's idea in genuinely new wording and structure. It still requires citation. | A {signal phrase} introduces a source's contribution. Follow evidence with explanation so readers understand its role in the argument.
Portfolio: Why Facts Matter in Research Argumentation | {Fact-checking} verifies a claim against suitable evidence. Repetition across websites does not guarantee independent confirmation. | A {citation} lets readers locate a source. It supports transparency but does not by itself prove the source is reliable. | A {revision} should narrow or change a claim when evidence does not support it. Protecting a preferred conclusion is not the goal of research.
The Research Process: Where to Look for Existing Sources | A {database} organizes searchable records, often with filters for subject, date, or publication type. Search results still need evaluation. | A {keyword} describes an important concept in the research question. Trying synonyms can reveal sources a first search misses. | A {primary source} offers direct evidence for the subject under study. A secondary source analyzes or interprets such evidence.
Annotated Student Sample: Research Log | A {research log} records searches, sources, and useful notes. It helps avoid repeating failed searches or losing the origin of an idea. | A {source note} should distinguish exact quotations from paraphrases and your own thoughts. Clear labels reduce accidental plagiarism. | A {search revision} changes keywords or scope in response to results. An empty search is feedback, not proof that no evidence exists.
Spotlight on … Ethical Research | {Attribution} credits ideas and wording from other people. It is required even when the source is freely available online. | {Plagiarism} presents another person's work or ideas as one's own. Changing a few words does not make an uncited passage original. | {Ethical research} also considers consent, privacy, and accurate representation. Correct citation does not excuse misrepresenting a source or exposing a participant.

Denying basic personal knowledge illustrates a form of control under slavery. | The separation from his {mother} shows how enslavement disrupted family relationships. The chapter connects personal experience with a wider oppressive system. | The narrator's {testimony} is evidence from lived experience. Read his specific observations carefully rather than treating the account as an abstract description of slavery.
Frederick Douglass · CHAPTER II | Douglass describes plantation {hierarchy}: enslaved people faced authority from owners and overseers. Power operated through work, surveillance, and punishment. | He explains that enslaved people's {songs} expressed suffering, not proof of happiness. His interpretation challenges what an outside listener might assume. | A reader must distinguish {appearance} from experience. The narrator explicitly corrects interpretations that hide slavery's cruelty.
Frederick Douglass · CHAPTER III | The chapter describes {surveillance} and punishment on Colonel Lloyd's plantation. Ordinary actions could expose enslaved people to severe consequences. | The episode about speaking of an enslaver reveals how {fear} shaped what people could safely say. Public statements might not express private belief. | Douglass uses specific {examples} to expose a system of arbitrary power. Connect each episode to the claim it supports, rather than remembering only its plot.
Nathaniel Hawthorne · I. THE PRISON DOOR | The opening places a {prison} near the center of a new Puritan settlement. This detail complicates the community's idealized picture of itself. | A {rosebush} grows beside the prison door. Its natural beauty contrasts with the harsh building and suggests a different response to suffering. | The chapter establishes {contrast} between punishment and compassion. Symbols invite interpretation, so support a reading with details rather than assigning one fixed meaning.
Nathaniel Hawthorne · II. THE MARKET-PLACE | Hester Prynne faces public {shaming} on a scaffold. The scene shows punishment as a communal spectacle, not only a private consequence. | The scarlet {letter} marks the community's judgment of Hester. Her elaborate embroidery complicates how that imposed sign is presented. | The crowd's {judgment} reveals social expectations and divisions. Different reactions show that the community does not speak with one completely uniform voice.
Nathaniel Hawthorne · III. THE RECOGNITION | Hester recognizes a man among the {crowd}. His concealed relationship to her introduces secrecy and a new source of conflict. | Authorities press Hester to reveal Pearl's {father}. Her refusal contrasts public demands for confession with hidden private knowledge. | {Dramatic tension} grows when readers learn that appearances conceal important relationships. Track who knows what rather than assuming all characters share the same information.
Herman Melville · CHAPTER 1. Loomings. | The narrator introduces himself as {Ishmael}. His first-person voice shapes the reader's access to his motives and observations. | Ishmael describes going to {sea} as a response to restlessness and dark moods. The chapter presents his stated motives, not an objective diagnosis. | The attraction of {water} becomes a recurring image. Connect his reflections with the chapter's movement toward a voyage.
Herman Melville · CHAPTER 2. The Carpet-Bag. | Ishmael arrives in {New Bedford} while seeking passage toward a whaling voyage. His limited money shapes his choices of lodging. | His search for an {inn} brings practical concerns into the story: cost, weather, and unfamiliar surroundings influence what happens next. | The chapter's {setting} prepares the transition from land to sea. Details of the town also reveal the narrator's habits of observation and judgment.
Herman Melville · CHAPTER 3. The Spouter-Inn. | At the {Spouter-Inn}, unfamiliar objects and stories shape Ishmael's expectations before he meets his roommate. Notice how little he initially knows. | The meeting with {Queequeg} exposes Ishmael's fear and prejudice. His judgments change as he encounters the actual person rather than imagined danger. | The shared {room} turns an uncomfortable practical situation into a developing relationship. Contrast assumptions made beforehand with behavior observed afterward.
Louisa May Alcott · CHAPTER ONE PLAYING PILGRIMS | The March sisters discuss {Christmas} and the things they cannot afford. Their different reactions introduce their personalities and family circumstances. | The absence of their {father}, who is serving in the war, gives the family's domestic concerns a wider historical setting. | Their plan to improve themselves uses {Pilgrim's Progress} as a model. The story connects imaginative play with moral growth, not merely entertainment.
Louisa May Alcott · CHAPTER TWO A MERRY CHRISTMAS | The sisters give their {breakfast} to a family in need. The action tests generosity more concretely than statements about being good. | Their homemade {performance} shows creativity and cooperation despite limited money. Each sister contributes in a different way. | The chapter links {generosity} with community relationships. Its rewards are part of the narrative's moral structure, not a real-world guarantee that kindness always earns material gifts.
Louisa May Alcott · CHAPTER THREE THE LAURENCE BOY | Jo meets {Laurie} at a social gathering. Their conversation begins a connection between the March family and their neighbor. | Jo's concerns about her {dress} reveal tension between social expectations and her personality. Compare how the characters act with what they initially assume about each other.
Edith Wharton · I. | The novel opens at the {opera}, where seating, attendance, and timing signal social standing in elite New York society. | {Newland Archer} observes and interprets the people around him. His viewpoint reveals both his confidence and his investment in social conventions. | {Social codes} govern behavior that might look merely decorative. Notice which rules are spoken and which everyone is expected to know without explanation.
Edith Wharton · II. | {Ellen Olenska}'s appearance draws attention because her circumstances and behavior do not fit the group's expectations. | Archer's connection to {May Welland} places him inside the established social order. His reactions are shaped by reputation as well as personal feeling. | {Convention} influences how characters judge others. Distinguish the narrative's exposure of these judgments from an endorsement of them.
Edith Wharton · III. | {Social reputation} shapes the consequences of personal decisions. Private relationships are closely watched by the surrounding community. | Archer's {engagement} connects family expectations with his own plans. Public recognition changes the meaning and stakes of a private relationship. | {Irony} emerges when social ideals differ from actual motives or behavior.
